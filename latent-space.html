01 / TOKENIZATION

Models don't read words.
They read tokens.

Before a language model sees your text, it gets cut into tokens: common words, pieces of words, punctuation, and spaces. Each token maps to an integer ID, and the model only ever works with those IDs. That's why models sometimes struggle to count the letters in "strawberry": they never see the individual letters.

How it works: Byte-Pair Encoding

Most tokenizers are built with BPE. It starts with single bytes, then repeatedly merges whichever adjacent pair shows up most often in a huge training corpus. After tens of thousands of merges, common words like the become a single token, while rare words get split into pieces like anti · dis · establish · ment.

Modern vocabularies have roughly 32k to 200k+ tokens. For English, one token averages about 4 characters, or about ¾ of a word. Other languages and code often need more tokens per word, which makes them more expensive to process.

Spaces are usually attached to the start of the following word. They show up here as ·.

02 / EMBEDDINGS

Meaning is a direction.

Each token ID is turned into a vector: a list of a few thousand numbers. During training, words that show up in similar contexts end up close together in this high-dimensional space. Directions in the space come to mean things too. The difference between "man" and "woman" points roughly the same way as the difference between "king" and "queen."

Real embeddings have thousands of dimensions. This map squeezes them down to 2D, much like a projection technique such as t-SNE or UMAP would. Search engines, recommendation systems, and RAG (section 10) all rely on this idea: things that are close in vector space have similar meanings.

03 / SELF-ATTENTION

Every token looks at
every other token.

Attention is the key idea behind the 2017 paper "Attention Is All You Need." For each token, the model computes a query ("what am I looking for?"), a key ("what do I contain?"), and a value ("what do I pass along?"). It compares each query against every key, and the resulting weights decide how much information flows from one token to another.

LAST WORD:

Click any token to make it the query. Notice how it attends to animal when the last word is "tired," and to street when it's "wide."

The math, in one line

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( QKᵀ / √d ) · V

Multi-head attention runs dozens of these in parallel, and each head learns its own pattern. Some heads track the previous token. Some link pronouns to what they refer to. Many dump extra attention onto the very first token, a pattern called an "attention sink."

Language models are causal: a token can only attend to earlier tokens, never later ones. That's why the top-right triangle of the heatmap is empty.

Attention costs O(n²) in the length of the context, which is why long context windows are expensive. Tricks like FlashAttention, grouped-query attention, sliding windows, and KV caching keep the cost manageable.

04 / NEXT-TOKEN PREDICTION

It's all just guessing the next token.

A language model outputs a probability for every token in its vocabulary. Then a token is sampled from that distribution, added to the text, and the whole process repeats. Temperature and top-p control how adventurous that sampling is.

Low temperature makes the distribution sharper, so the model almost always picks the top choice. That's deterministic and safe, but it can get repetitive. High temperature flattens it out, so even "banana" gets a chance.

Top-p keeps only the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities add up to p, then renormalizes. This cuts off the long tail of unlikely tokens. Grayed-out bars have been removed.

The yellow line under each bar is an empirical histogram. Click "Sample 1,000×" to watch randomness match the distribution.

06 / MIXTURE OF EXPERTS

Huge model. Small bill.

In a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model, each feed-forward layer is split into many smaller "expert" networks. A small router sends each token to only its top-k experts. The model can have hundreds of billions of parameters in total while using only a fraction of them for each token.

8×
Total FFN params
2×
Active per token

Each token goes through only 2 of the 8 experts, so it costs about as much compute as a model a quarter of the size, while the model still gets to store the knowledge of all 8.

For example, Mixtral 8×7B has about 47B parameters in total but only about 13B active per token. DeepSeek-V3 has 671B in total and about 37B active. Several frontier labs are widely believed to use MoE in their largest models.

The tricky part is load balancing: if the router keeps choosing the same experts, the rest go to waste. Experts rarely end up specializing in clean topics like "French" or "math." What they learn is usually much more abstract.

07 / DIFFUSION MODELS

Sculpting images out of noise.

Image and video generators like Stable Diffusion, FLUX, Imagen, and Sora are trained on a simple task. Take a real image, add a random amount of noise, and ask the network to predict the noise. To generate a new image, start from pure static and remove a little predicted noise at a time, guided by your text prompt.

The forward process

xt = √ᾱt · x0 + √(1 − ᾱt) · ε

At timestep t, the image is a mix of the clean picture x₀ and Gaussian noise ε. The slider shows that mix exactly, using a cosine noise schedule.

Generating an image runs this process in reverse. A neural network, usually a U-Net or a Diffusion Transformer (DiT), predicts the noise, removes some of it, and repeats for 20 to 50 steps. Most models work in a compressed latent space rather than on raw pixels, which is why it's called latent diffusion.

Newer flow-matching models learn straighter paths from noise to image, so they can generate in fewer steps.

08 / HOW A MODEL IS MADE

From autocomplete to assistant.

A raw pretrained model is a brilliant but unfocused autocomplete. Several more stages of training turn it into a helpful, careful assistant. The most recent stage teaches it to think before it answers. Click a stage to see what its training data looks like.

A bat and a ball cost $1.10 in total. The bat costs $1.00 more than the ball. How much does the ball cost?

Reasoning models and test-time compute

Reasoning models such as OpenAI's o-series, DeepSeek-R1, and the "thinking" modes of Claude and Gemini are trained with reinforcement learning on problems with checkable answers, like math or code that has tests. The model is rewarded only when its final answer is right. Along the way, it learns to write out long chains of thought: breaking problems into steps, checking its work, and backtracking when it's wrong.

This opened a new way to scale. Instead of only training bigger models, you can let a model think longer at inference time. More thinking tokens often means better answers on hard problems.

09 / SCALING LAWS

Bigger is predictably better.

One of the most surprising findings in AI: a model's training loss falls along a smooth power law as you add compute, data, and parameters. Labs fit these curves on small runs to predict how a run that's 100× larger will turn out before spending the money.

The Chinchilla paper (2022) found that for a fixed compute budget, the best results come from about 20 training tokens per parameter, with compute ≈ 6 × params × tokens. Many models today are deliberately "overtrained" on far more tokens than that, because smaller models are cheaper to run once deployed.

*Assumes about 40% utilization of an H100's roughly 1 PFLOP/s of dense BF16 throughput. The curve is illustrative, not fitted to real data.

10 / RETRIEVAL-AUGMENTED GENERATION

Give the model an open book.

Models only know what was in their training data, and they sometimes hallucinate. RAG fixes this by searching your documents first, using embeddings from section 02, and pasting the most relevant passages into the prompt. Agents take this further: the model decides on its own when to search, run code, or call other tools, and loops until the task is done.
