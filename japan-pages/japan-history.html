序

An Archipelago of Reinvention日本史概観

Japan's history keeps returning to one pattern: an island society takes in ideas from outside (rice farming, Chinese writing, Buddhism, firearms, Western law, American mass culture), slowly makes them its own, and then turns inward to refine them, sometimes for centuries.

Officially the state descends from a sun goddess. In practice it was shaped by migration across the Korea Strait, centralized on the Tang Chinese model, and then for nearly seven hundred years run largely by warriors, the bushi, while emperors in Kyoto reigned without ruling. In the 19th century Japan went from feudal isolation to industrial empire within about fifty years. It then lost that empire in catastrophic war and rebuilt itself as a pacifist democracy and, for a time, the world's second-largest economy. Today it is an aging, wealthy, technologically sophisticated society whose cultural exports, from Zen and sushi to anime and video games, are known around the world.

Land, Sea & Regions地理と地方

Japan is an arc of islands roughly 3,000 km long off the east coast of Asia, with a land area of about 378,000 km². The four main islands are Honshū, Hokkaidō, Kyūshū and Shikoku, followed by the Ryūkyū chain to the southwest. Roughly three-quarters of the country is mountainous or hilly, so people, rice paddies and cities crowd onto narrow coastal plains such as the Kantō, Nōbi and Kinai. The country sits where four tectonic plates meet on the Pacific "Ring of Fire". It has more than 100 active volcanoes and experiences around a tenth of the world's strong earthquakes. Its tallest peak, Mount Fuji (3,776 m), is itself a dormant stratovolcano that last erupted in 1707.

Geography shaped the history. The Korea Strait was close enough for migration and trade but wide enough to deter invasion, and the only serious attempt before 1945 (by the Mongols in the 13th century) failed. The mountainous interior favored regional lords. The Seto Inland Sea served as the main highway linking Kyūshū to the Kinai heartland around Nara, Kyoto and Osaka.

古

Prehistory & the Age of the Gods先史時代と神話

The Paleolithic 旧石器時代c. 38,000 – c. 14,000 BCE (dates debated)

Humans reached the archipelago at least 38,000 years ago, when lower sea levels during the last Ice Age narrowed the straits. Some routes may even have joined Hokkaidō to Sakhalin and the Asian mainland. Japan's Paleolithic was only recognized in 1949, when the amateur archaeologist Aizawa Tadahiro found stone tools at Iwajuku (Gunma). These early people made some of the world's oldest known edge-ground stone axes, crossed open water to reach islands such as Kōzushima to collect obsidian, and hunted Naumann's elephants and giant deer. The field was badly shaken in 2000, when the archaeologist Fujimura Shin'ichi was filmed planting artifacts. His "discoveries" of supposedly ancient sites were thrown out, and decades of chronology had to be reassessed.

Jōmon 縄文時代c. 14,000 – c. 300 BCE

The Jōmon ("cord-marked") period is named for pottery decorated by pressing twisted cords into wet clay. Japanese ceramics are among the oldest in the world: shards from sites such as Ōdai Yamamoto I are dated to roughly 16,000 years ago. Unusually, the Jōmon were largely settled hunter-gatherer-fishers. They lived in pit-dwelling villages, managed chestnut groves, gathered shellfish (leaving huge shell middens) and fished the rich coastal waters, all without full agriculture.

Sannai-Maruyama

Near Aomori, a Middle Jōmon settlement (c. 3900–2200 BCE) with long houses, storage pits, burial rows and the post holes of a large six-pillar timber structure. It is part of the "Jōmon Prehistoric Sites in Northern Japan" UNESCO listing (2021).

Dogū & flame pots

Fired-clay figurines (dogū), often female and often deliberately broken, suggest fertility or healing rites. The spectacular Middle Jōmon "flame-style" vessels (kaen doki) from Niigata are among the most dramatic ceramics of the prehistoric world.

Population estimates (very approximate) suggest about 20,000 people in the Initial Jōmon, a peak of perhaps 250,000 around 3000–2000 BCE, and then a decline as the climate cooled. Genetic studies show that modern mainland Japanese carry a modest Jōmon ancestry. That share is much higher among the Ainu and is also elevated among Ryukyuans.

Yayoi 弥生時代c. 300 BCE – c. 250 CE (some radiocarbon dates start c. 900–1000 BCE)

Wet-rice farming, bronze and iron, and new types of pottery arrived in northern Kyūshū with migrants from the Korean Peninsula. Over several centuries the new way of life spread northeast. Rice brought surpluses, land disputes, social ranks and war: Yayoi villages such as Yoshinogari (Saga) were ringed by moats and watchtowers. Bronze bells (dōtaku), mirrors and weapons served as ritual objects.

At this point Japan enters written history, but only through Chinese records. The Book of Han mentions the people of Wa (倭) divided into "more than a hundred countries". In 57 CE the Later Han emperor gave a gold seal to the king of Na, and a seal matching the description was found on Shikanoshima island in 1784. The Records of Wei (3rd century) describe Queen Himiko of Yamatai, a shaman ruler who "occupied herself with magic and sorcery" and sent envoys to the Wei court in 239. Whether Yamatai lay in northern Kyūshū or in the Kinai region (perhaps at Makimuku in Nara) is one of Japanese history's longest-running debates.

Founding myths 神代As recorded in the Kojiki (712) and Nihon Shoki (720)

The myth

The sibling deities Izanagi and Izanami stir the primordial sea with a jeweled spear, and the brine that drips from it forms the first island. Their children include the sun goddess Amaterasu, the moon god Tsukuyomi and the storm god Susanoo. Angered by Susanoo, Amaterasu hides in a cave and plunges the world into darkness until the other gods lure her out with a mirror and a bawdy dance. Her grandson Ninigi descends to earth carrying the three regalia: the mirror Yata no Kagami, the sword Kusanagi and the jewel Yasakani no Magatama. Ninigi's great-grandson Jimmu marches east from Kyūshū and is enthroned at Kashihara in Yamato, traditionally on 11 February 660 BCE.

The archaeology

In 660 BCE the archipelago was in the late Jōmon or earliest Yayoi period, with no state and no writing. The date appears to have been calculated by 8th-century compilers counting back a 1,260-year cycle of the Chinese sexagenary calendar from 601 CE, a year of particular cosmological significance. Emperors before roughly the 5th–6th century are considered legendary or semi-legendary. Kinmei (r. c. 539–571) is generally the earliest emperor whose dates historians can verify. The myths themselves were political texts, assembled to justify the Yamato royal line's claim to rule.

Myth in modern politics. From 1872 until 1945, the State made 11 February a national holiday, Kigensetsu, and taught Jimmu's founding as literal history. The holiday was abolished during the Occupation and revived in 1966 as Kenkoku Kinen no Hi (National Foundation Day). The vaguer name was a compromise over its mythological basis.
朝

Kofun, Asuka, Nara & Heian古墳・飛鳥・奈良・平安

Kofun period 古墳時代c. 250 – 538 CE

The period is named after its kofun, monumental burial mounds. The largest were "keyhole-shaped" (zenpō-kōen-fun), and their spread across Japan traces the reach of a confederation centered on the Yamato and Kawachi plains. The biggest, the Daisen Kofun in Sakai and traditionally the tomb of Emperor Nintoku, is about 486 m long, one of the largest graves on Earth. The Mozu–Furuichi Kofun Group became a UNESCO site in 2019. The mounds were ringed with haniwa, hollow clay figures of warriors, horses, houses and shrine maidens. Society was organized into clans (uji) with hereditary ranks (kabane). Yamato was deeply involved on the Korean Peninsula, especially with Baekje and the Gaya confederacy, and imported iron, horses, scribes and artisans.

Asuka period 飛鳥時代538 (or 552) – 710

Buddhism arrived when the king of Baekje sent an image and sutras to the Yamato court, in 538 or 552 depending on the source. Whether to accept the foreign god split the court: the pro-Buddhist Soga clan defeated the conservative Mononobe in 587. Under Empress Suiko (r. 592–628), the first reigning empress whose rule is historically attested, her nephew Prince Shōtoku (574–622) is credited with the twelve cap ranks (603), the Seventeen-Article Constitution (604), a moral code beginning "Harmony is to be valued", and missions to Sui China. One letter famously addressed the Sui emperor from "the Son of Heaven where the sun rises". Hōryū-ji, which he founded, contains the world's oldest surviving wooden buildings. Modern historians think much of Shōtoku's legend was embellished after his death.

In the Isshi Incident of 645, Prince Naka no Ōe (later Emperor Tenji) and Nakatomi no Kamatari assassinated Soga no Iruka at court. Kamatari was later given the surname Fujiwara, founding the most powerful noble family in Japanese history. The Taika Reforms (from 646) adopted the Chinese model: in principle, all land and people belonged to the sovereign, with census registers, equal-field allotments and provincial administration. Defeat by Tang and Silla forces at Baekgang (663) spurred fortress-building. After the Jinshin War (672), Emperor Tenmu strengthened the throne, and by around 700 the titles Tennō (heavenly sovereign) and the country name Nihon ("origin of the sun") were in use. The Taihō Code (701) completed the ritsuryō legal state.

Nara period 奈良時代710 – 794

The first permanent capital, Heijō-kyō (Nara), was laid out on a grid modeled on the Tang capital Chang'an. The period saw the compilation of Japan's earliest surviving books: the Kojiki (712), the Nihon Shoki (720), provincial gazetteers (fudoki), and the Man'yōshū (compiled after 759), an anthology of about 4,500 poems by everyone from emperors to frontier guards. After a devastating smallpox epidemic (735–737) that killed perhaps a quarter to a third of the population, Emperor Shōmu ordered a provincial temple in every province and the colossal bronze Great Buddha of Tōdai-ji, dedicated in 752. The blind Chinese monk Ganjin (Jianzhen) arrived in 753 after five failed voyages. The growing political weight of Buddhist institutions culminated in the monk Dōkyō's attempt to take the throne. That alarm is one reason the capital eventually moved away from Nara's temples.

Heian period 平安時代794 – 1185

Emperor Kanmu moved the capital to Heian-kyō, the "capital of peace and tranquility", which remained the imperial seat (as Kyoto) until 1869. The monks Saichō (Tendai, on Mount Hiei) and Kūkai (Shingon, on Mount Kōya) brought new esoteric Buddhist schools from China. In 894, on the advice of the scholar Sugawara no Michizane, official embassies to the declining Tang were halted, and an inward-looking kokufū ("national style") culture flourished.

The Fujiwara regency

The Fujiwara ruled through marriage politics. They married daughters to emperors, then governed as sesshō (regent for a child emperor) and kampaku (chancellor for an adult). Fujiwara no Michinaga (966–1028) was father-in-law to four emperors and grandfather to three, and boasted that the world was his "like the full moon, unwaning." From 1086, retired emperors governing from their cloisters (insei) took power back from the regents.

Literature & kana

The new kana syllabaries made Japanese-language prose possible, and much of it was written by court women. Murasaki Shikibu's The Tale of Genji (c. 1008), often called the world's first novel, and Sei Shōnagon's Pillow Book are the masterpieces. The Kokinshū (905) set the standard for court poetry for a thousand years. Its kana preface by Ki no Tsurayuki declares that Japanese poetry "takes the human heart as its seed."

Meanwhile, in the provinces, tax-exempt private estates (shōen) eroded the central treasury, and armed provincial families rose to defend and exploit them. The greatest were the Taira (Heike) and the Minamoto (Genji), both branches descended from emperors. After the Hōgen (1156) and Heiji (1160) disturbances, Taira no Kiyomori dominated the court. The Genpei War (1180–1185) ended at the naval battle of Dan-no-ura, where the child Emperor Antoku drowned with his Taira kin. The Tale of the Heike remembers the war as a meditation on impermanence: "The sound of the Gion Shōja bells echoes the impermanence of all things…"

鎌

The Kamakura Shogunate & Kenmu Restoration鎌倉幕府・建武の新政

Kamakura period 鎌倉時代1185 – 1333

Minamoto no Yoritomo (1147–1199) set up a military government, the bakufu ("tent government"), at Kamakura, his eastern base far from Kyoto's intrigues. In 1185 the court allowed him to appoint military governors (shugo) to provinces and stewards (jitō) to estates, which many historians now treat as the real founding of the shogunate. In 1192 he received the title sei-i taishōgun ("great general who subdues the barbarians"). For the first time Japan had a dual government: the imperial court in Kyoto kept its ceremony and prestige, while the warrior government in Kamakura held the land and the swords. Yoritomo, suspicious to the end, destroyed his own brilliant half-brother Minamoto no Yoshitsune, the hero of Dan-no-ura, who died at Hiraizumi in 1189 and became Japan's archetypal tragic hero.

The Hōjō regents

After Yoritomo's death (1199), real power passed to the family of his widow Hōjō Masako, the formidable "nun shogun". The Hōjō ruled as shikken (regents) for puppet shoguns, at first Fujiwara and later imperial princes. So by the 13th century Japan had an emperor overshadowed by a retired emperor, a shogun controlled by a regent, and a regent who was sometimes guided by retired Hōjō elders. In the Jōkyū War (1221), Retired Emperor Go-Toba tried to overthrow Kamakura. He was crushed and exiled, and the bakufu set up a deputy office (Rokuhara Tandai) to watch the court. In 1232 the regent Hōjō Yasutoki issued the Goseibai Shikimoku, Japan's first warrior law code, based on precedent and "what is reasonable."

The Mongol invasions and the "kamikaze"

Kublai Khan, ruler of the Mongol Yuan dynasty, demanded submission, and the young regent Hōjō Tokimune refused. In 1274 (the Bun'ei invasion), a Mongol–Korean fleet of perhaps 900 ships ravaged Tsushima and Iki and landed at Hakata Bay. Samurai who expected single combat met massed formations, poisoned arrows and exploding bombs (tetsuhau). The invaders withdrew after a storm. In 1281 (the Kōan invasion) two much larger fleets arrived, possibly over 100,000 men including a southern Chinese armada. They found Hakata defended by a stone wall, and samurai raided the ships by night. After weeks of stalemate a typhoon destroyed much of the fleet. The storms were hailed as kamikaze ("divine winds"), proof that Japan was a land of the gods. Underwater archaeology off Takashima suggests many Yuan ships were hastily built river craft.

Why victory weakened Kamakura. A defensive war produced no conquered land to hand out as rewards. Vassals (gokenin) who had paid for their own service went unrewarded and fell into debt, and debt-cancellation decrees (tokusei, 1297) undermined credit. Combined with factional infighting, this left the Hōjō brittle.

Kamakura Buddhism

The era produced the popular, single-practice schools that still dominate Japanese religion: Hōnen's Pure Land (Jōdo, 1175) and his disciple Shinran's True Pure Land (Jōdo Shinshū), both relying on the nenbutsu invocation of Amida; Nichiren's devotion to the Lotus Sutra; and Zen, brought from Song China by Eisai (Rinzai, who also popularized tea) and Dōgen (Sōtō). Sculptors such as Unkei created vigorous, realistic works, among them the guardian kings of Tōdai-ji's Great South Gate. The Great Buddha of Kamakura was cast in the mid-13th century.

The Kenmu Restoration 建武の新政1333 – 1336

The ambitious Emperor Go-Daigo plotted to restore direct imperial rule. Exiled to the Oki Islands, he escaped, and Kamakura's own generals turned on it. Ashikaga Takauji seized Kyoto's Rokuhara office, and Nitta Yoshisada stormed Kamakura in 1333, where the last Hōjō regent and hundreds of followers killed themselves. Go-Daigo's restoration, however, favored courtiers over the warriors who had won it. Takauji rebelled, defeated the loyalist Kusunoki Masashige at Minatogawa (1336), and installed a rival emperor. Masashige, who knowingly fought a hopeless battle, later became the model of selfless loyalty to the throne, a symbol heavily used by prewar nationalists.

室

The Ashikaga Shogunate & the Warring States室町幕府・戦国時代

Muromachi period 室町時代1336/1338 – 1573

Ashikaga Takauji became shogun in 1338 and placed his government in Kyoto, later in the Muromachi district, which gave the period its name. Go-Daigo fled south to Yoshino, starting the era of the Northern and Southern Courts (Nanboku-chō, 1336–1392), when two rival imperial lines each claimed the regalia. The third shogun, Ashikaga Yoshimitsu (1358–1408), reunited the courts in 1392 on terms the south never saw honored. He accepted the title "King of Japan" from the Ming emperor to open lucrative tally trade, and built the Golden Pavilion (Kinkaku-ji). (In the Meiji era, the government declared the Southern Court the legitimate line, even though the current imperial family descends from the Northern Court.)

The Ashikaga were never as strong as the Kamakura regime. They depended on great provincial shugo lords, such as the Hosokawa, Shiba, Hatakeyama and Yamana, who increasingly built up their own territories. Even so, Muromachi culture shaped what the world now calls "traditional Japan": Noh theater (Kan'ami and Zeami, patronized by Yoshimitsu), ink painting (Sesshū), Zen rock gardens, the tea ceremony, ikebana, and the shoin-zukuri style of architecture with tatami, alcoves and sliding doors. The eighth shogun, Yoshimasa, was politically hopeless but presided over the refined Higashiyama culture of the Silver Pavilion (Ginkaku-ji).

The Ōnin War (1467–1477)

A succession dispute in the shogunal house, tangled with feuds inside the Hatakeyama and Shiba families, drew the Hosokawa (Eastern Army) and Yamana (Western Army) into ten years of war, fought largely in the streets of Kyoto. The capital was reduced to ashes and the war ended with no victor. The shogun's authority never recovered: power passed to whoever could hold territory by force, and the provinces broke into autonomous domains.

Sengoku period 戦国時代c. 1467 – 1568 (fighting continued to 1615)

The "Warring States" period is named after China's era of the same name. It was the age of gekokujō, "the low overthrowing the high": retainers toppled lords, and new warlords called daimyō ("great names") ruled domains as independent states, with their own law codes, castle towns, mines and tax surveys. Legendary rivals such as Takeda Shingen and Uesugi Kenshin fought five battles at Kawanakajima. Leagues of commoners also seized power: the Ikkō-ikki of Jōdo Shinshū believers ruled Kaga province for nearly a century, and Sakai was a self-governing merchant city.

Europeans arrive. In 1543 Portuguese traders shipwrecked on Tanegashima brought matchlock guns. Within decades Japanese smiths were producing tens of thousands, and firearms transformed warfare. The Jesuit Francis Xavier landed at Kagoshima in 1549. By about 1600 there may have been 300,000 Japanese Christians, and some daimyō, the "Christian daimyō", converted too. Words like pan (bread) and tempura are Portuguese legacies.

Azuchi–Momoyama: The Three Unifiers安土桃山時代

織田がつき 羽柴がこねし天下餅 座りしままに食うは徳川"Oda pounded the rice, Hashiba [Hideyoshi] kneaded the cake, and Tokugawa sat down and ate it."Edo-period satirical verse

Oda Nobunaga 織田信長

1534–1582

A minor lord from Owari who destroyed the far larger Imagawa army at Okehazama (1560), entered Kyoto in 1568 with the would-be shogun Ashikaga Yoshiaki, then expelled him in 1573, ending the Ashikaga shogunate. He burned the Enryaku-ji temple complex (1571), defeated the Takeda with massed musket volleys at Nagashino (1575), built the soaring Azuchi Castle, and promoted free markets (rakuichi rakuza). Betrayed by his general Akechi Mitsuhide, he died at Honnō-ji in 1582.

Toyotomi Hideyoshi 豊臣秀吉

1537–1598

Born a commoner, he rose to be Nobunaga's best general. He avenged his lord within two weeks at Yamazaki and completed unification with the fall of Odawara (1590). Barred by birth from the shogun title, he ruled as kampaku and taikō. His sword hunt (1588), nationwide land surveys and an edict fixing social status separated warriors from farmers. He expelled missionaries (1587), crucified the 26 Martyrs (1597), built Osaka Castle, and launched two disastrous invasions of Korea (1592–98).

Tokugawa Ieyasu 徳川家康

1543–1616

A patient survivor, raised as a hostage in childhood and an ally of Nobunaga, who accepted relocation to the Kantō in 1590 and built up the then-modest fishing town of Edo. After Hideyoshi's death he outmaneuvered the regency council and won the decisive battle of Sekigahara (1600). He became shogun in 1603, abdicated in 1605 to secure his dynasty, and finally destroyed the Toyotomi at the sieges of Osaka (1614–15).

The Imjin War: invasions of Korea (1592–1598)

Hideyoshi aimed to conquer Ming China through Korea. In 1592 roughly 150,000 troops took Seoul and Pyongyang within weeks. Then Admiral Yi Sun-sin's fleet, including his famous "turtle ships", cut Japanese supply lines, Korean "righteous armies" rose up, and Ming armies intervened. A second invasion in 1597 bogged down in the south and was abandoned on Hideyoshi's death in 1598. The war devastated Korea. Japanese troops took noses as war trophies (the Mimizuka mound in Kyoto) and carried off thousands of captives, including potters who founded celebrated ceramic traditions such as Arita and Satsuma ware. The war remains a painful memory in Korea.

Momoyama splendor

Fortress-palaces such as Himeji, Osaka and Fushimi were decorated with gold-leaf screens by Kanō Eitoku and Hasegawa Tōhaku. At the opposite pole, the tea master Sen no Rikyū perfected wabi-cha in tiny, rustic two-mat tea rooms. Hideyoshi staged both: the portable Golden Tea Room and the vast Kitano Grand Tea Gathering of 1587. In 1591 he ordered Rikyū to commit suicide, for reasons that are still debated.

江

The Tokugawa Shogunate: 265 Years of Peace江戸時代

Sekigahara and the new order 関ヶ原21 October 1600 (Keichō 5, 9th month, 15th day)

In a fog-bound valley in Mino, Ieyasu's Eastern Army fought Ishida Mitsunari's Western Army, perhaps 160,000 men in all. The battle turned when Kobayakawa Hideaki defected mid-battle. The distinction between lords who had sided with Ieyasu before Sekigahara (fudai, hereditary vassals) and those who submitted only afterward (tozama, "outside" lords) shaped politics for 250 years. In the 1860s, two great tozama domains, Satsuma and Chōshū, would bring the shogunate down.

The machinery of control 幕藩体制The bakuhan system

Sankin-kōtai (alternate attendance)

Formalized in 1635 under the third shogun Iemitsu. Daimyō had to spend alternate years in Edo, and their wives and heirs lived there permanently as hostages. The splendid processions drained domain treasuries, spread culture and commerce along the Tōkaidō and other highways, and helped make Edo one of the largest cities in the world, with perhaps a million people by the 18th century.

Laws and limits

The Buke shohatto (Laws for Military Households, 1615) regulated marriages, castle repairs and conduct. A parallel code bound the imperial court. The shogun directly controlled about a quarter of the land, the major cities and the mines. Some 260–280 domains (han) kept their own administration and armies, giving a system that was both centralized and decentralized.

Sakoku: the "closed country"

The Shimabara Rebellion (1637–38), a revolt of overtaxed, largely Christian peasants led by the teenage Amakusa Shirō, was crushed with heavy loss of life. It confirmed the bakufu's fear of Christianity. A series of edicts (1633–1639) banned Christianity, barred Japanese from going abroad or returning, and expelled the Portuguese. The Dutch, who were Protestant and interested only in trade, were confined from 1641 to the tiny artificial island of Dejima in Nagasaki. Historians now stress that "sakoku" (a term coined in 1801) was controlled contact rather than total isolation, conducted through four "gateways": the Dutch and Chinese at Nagasaki, Korea via Tsushima, the Ryūkyū Kingdom via Satsuma (which had invaded it in 1609), and the Ainu via Matsumae.

The status order (mibunsei)

Neo-Confucian ideology ranked society as shi-nō-kō-shō: samurai, farmers, artisans, merchants. Samurai (about 6–7% of the population) became salaried bureaucrats living in castle towns. Peasants, about 80%, paid rice taxes collectively through their villages. Merchants were formally lowest but grew rich, and many samurai fell into debt to them. Court nobles, clergy and outcaste groups (eta and hinin, whose descendants, the burakumin, still face discrimination) stood outside the four classes. In practice the boundaries were more fluid than the theory suggests.

The floating world

Peace, roads and money created a vibrant urban commoner culture (chōnin bunka), centered on the pleasure quarters and theaters of the ukiyo, the "floating world". In the Genroku era (1688–1704) Matsuo Bashō raised haikai poetry to high art, Ihara Saikaku wrote racy novels of merchants and lovers, and Chikamatsu Monzaemon wrote double-suicide plays for the puppet theater. Woodblock prints evolved from Hishikawa Moronobu's monochrome sheets to Suzuki Harunobu's full-color "brocade prints" (1765), Utamaro's beauties, Sharaku's actors, and finally Hokusai's Great Wave (c. 1831) and Hiroshige's Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō (1833–34). Literacy rose sharply thanks to temple schools (terakoya). Some estimates put male literacy at 40–50% by the 1860s, high by the standards of the time.

Strains, reforms and new ideas (18th–19th c.)
  • The 47 Rōnin (1701–03): masterless samurai avenged their lord and were ordered to commit suicide. Retold as Chūshingura, the story became the archetype of loyalty.
  • Reforms: the Kyōhō (Yoshimune, 1716–45), Kansei (Matsudaira Sadanobu, 1787–93) and Tenpō (Mizuno Tadakuni, 1841–43) reforms all tried to restore austerity and agrarian finance, with diminishing success.
  • Famines: the Kyōhō, Tenmei (1780s) and Tenpō (1830s) famines brought peasant uprisings (ikki) and urban riots. In 1837 the former official Ōshio Heihachirō led a revolt in Osaka.
  • Rangaku ("Dutch learning"): Sugita Genpaku's translated anatomy text (Kaitai Shinsho, 1774) opened Western medicine and science to Japanese scholars.
  • Kokugaku ("National learning"): Motoori Norinaga studied the Kojiki and Genji to recover a "pure" Japanese spirit. Together with the Mito school, this fed later emperor-centered nationalism.
  • Population rose from perhaps 12–17 million in 1600 to around 30 million by 1720, then stayed nearly flat until the 1850s.

The Black Ships

On 8 July 1853, US Commodore Matthew C. Perry steamed into Edo Bay off Uraga with four warships, two of them steam frigates belching black smoke, the kurofune. He delivered President Fillmore's letter and returned in February 1854 with a larger squadron. The Convention of Kanagawa (March 1854) opened Shimoda and Hakodate. Consul Townsend Harris then won the Harris Treaty (1858), the first of the "unequal treaties", which gave foreigners extraterritoriality and fixed low tariffs. Signing them without imperial consent shattered the shogunate's legitimacy.

明

Bakumatsu & the Meiji Restoration幕末・明治維新

Bakumatsu: the end of the bakufu 幕末1853 – 1868

The treaties set off a political earthquake. The rallying cry sonnō jōi ("revere the emperor, expel the barbarians") united radical young samurai, the shishi. The great councilor Ii Naosuke, who signed the Harris Treaty and purged opponents in the Ansei Purge, was cut down outside Edo Castle's Sakurada Gate in 1860. After Satsuma samurai killed the British merchant Charles Richardson (the Namamugi Incident, 1862), Britain bombarded Kagoshima (1863), and Western fleets pounded Chōshū at Shimonoseki (1863–64). Both domains concluded that expulsion was impossible and that Japan must first modernize. Brokered partly by Sakamoto Ryōma of Tosa, the former enemies formed the Satsuma–Chōshū Alliance (1866). The last shogun, Tokugawa Yoshinobu, returned his governing authority to the emperor in November 1867 (taisei hōkan), hoping to lead a new council. On 3 January 1868, however, palace coup leaders proclaimed the "restoration of imperial rule".

The Boshin War (1868–69) followed. Pro-imperial forces won at Toba–Fushimi. Saigō Takamori and the shogunal official Katsu Kaishū negotiated the bloodless surrender of Edo. Aizu was besieged, and the teenage Byakkotai killed themselves. The last holdouts declared the short-lived "Republic of Ezo" at Hakodate's Goryōkaku fortress before surrendering in 1869.

The Meiji era 明治時代1868 – 1912 · Emperor Mutsuhito (Meiji)

The teenage emperor's Charter Oath (1868) promised that "knowledge shall be sought throughout the world." The emperor moved to Edo, renamed Tokyo ("Eastern Capital"). An oligarchy of young Satsuma, Chōshū, Tosa and Hizen samurai, including Ōkubo Toshimichi, Kido Takayoshi, Itō Hirobumi and Yamagata Aritomo, then dismantled the old order with breathtaking speed:

  • 1871: domains abolished and replaced by prefectures (haihan chiken). The Iwakura Mission (1871–73) toured the US and Europe for nearly two years.
  • 1872–73: universal education ordinance, first railway (Shinbashi–Yokohama), the Gregorian calendar, military conscription, and land-tax reform.
  • 1876: samurai stipends commuted and public sword-wearing banned. The Satsuma Rebellion (1877), led by Saigō Takamori, was the last samurai revolt, crushed by a conscript army.
  • 1879: the Ryūkyū Kingdom annexed as Okinawa Prefecture. Hokkaidō colonized, with Ainu assimilation policies (1899 Former Aborigines Protection Act).
  • 1889: the Meiji Constitution, modeled on Prussia's, declared the emperor "sacred and inviolable". The Imperial Diet first met in 1890, the same year as the Imperial Rescript on Education.
  • Industrialization: state-built model factories (Tomioka Silk Mill, 1872), then private zaibatsu conglomerates such as Mitsui, Mitsubishi and Sumitomo. Silk and cotton textiles drove early growth.

Abroad, Japan defeated Qing China in the First Sino-Japanese War (1894–95), taking Taiwan. It allied with Britain (1902), then stunned the world by defeating Russia (1904–05), sinking the Baltic Fleet at Tsushima. It was the first modern victory of an Asian power over a European one. Korea became a protectorate (1905) and was annexed in 1910. By 1911, when tariff autonomy was restored, the unequal treaties had been fully revised, and Japan was recognized as a great power and an empire.

Civilization and Enlightenment. Bunmei kaika brought gas lamps on the Ginza, brick buildings, beef stew and Western haircuts. The educator Fukuzawa Yukichi, whose face appeared on the ¥10,000 note until 2024, preached independence and practical learning, and in 1885 an essay attributed to him urged Japan to "leave Asia".
昭

Taishō Democracy & the Road to War大正デモクラシーと戦争

Taishō era 大正時代1912 – 1926 · Emperor Yoshihito

With the sickly Taishō Emperor out of public view, party politics flourished. Japan joined the Allies in World War I, seized Germany's Pacific and Chinese holdings, and pressed the Twenty-One Demands on China (1915). The wartime boom ended in the nationwide Rice Riots of 1918, after which Hara Takashi became the first commoner and party-based prime minister. He was assassinated in 1921. Japan sat among the "Big Five" at Versailles, but its proposal for a racial equality clause was rejected. The Great Kantō Earthquake (1 September 1923) and fires killed about 105,000 people. In the chaos, vigilantes and police massacred thousands of Koreans as well as Chinese and leftists. In 1925 universal male suffrage passed together with the repressive Peace Preservation Law. Urban culture boomed with "modern girls" (moga), cafés, jazz, cinema and radio.

Early Shōwa: militarism and war 昭和前期1926 – 1945 · Emperor Hirohito

The 1927 financial panic and the Great Depression devastated farming villages and discredited the parties. In 1931 Kwantung Army officers staged the Mukden Incident and seized Manchuria, creating the puppet state of Manchukuo (1932). Japan walked out of the League of Nations in 1933. Terrorism by young officers and ultranationalists killed Prime Minister Inukai Tsuyoshi (15 May 1932) and several senior officials in the attempted coup of 26 February 1936. The coup failed, but the army's political weight grew.

The Marco Polo Bridge Incident (July 1937) escalated into full-scale war with China. After Nanjing fell, Japanese troops carried out the Nanjing Massacre (December 1937–January 1938), killing tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands of civilians and prisoners. The scale is disputed, but the atrocity is not. Other wartime crimes included forced labor, Unit 731's biological experiments, and the system of "comfort women" forced into military brothels, which remains a live diplomatic issue. Japan joined the Axis (Tripartite Pact, 1940) and moved into French Indochina. When the US, Britain and the Netherlands answered with an oil embargo, Tokyo chose war: Pearl Harbor and simultaneous attacks across Southeast Asia (7–8 December 1941) launched the Pacific War under the banner of a "Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere."

After rapid early conquests, from Hong Kong and Singapore to the Philippines and Burma, the tide turned at Midway (June 1942) and Guadalcanal. Island-hopping US forces brought bombers within range of Japan. The firebombing of Tokyo on 9–10 March 1945 killed perhaps 100,000 people in a single night. The Battle of Okinawa (April–June 1945) killed over 200,000 people, roughly half of them Okinawan civilians.

Hiroshima and Nagasaki. On 6 August 1945 the uranium bomb "Little Boy" destroyed Hiroshima. On 9 August the plutonium bomb "Fat Man" struck Nagasaki, the same day the Soviet Union invaded Manchuria. By the end of 1945 an estimated 140,000 people had died in Hiroshima and 70,000 in Nagasaki, and many more died later from injuries and radiation. Survivors (hibakusha) became central to the global anti-nuclear movement. Their organization, Nihon Hidankyō, received the Nobel Peace Prize in 2024. On 15 August the emperor's recorded broadcast announced surrender, telling subjects to "endure the unendurable." The formal signing took place aboard USS Missouri on 2 September 1945. About 2.6–3.1 million Japanese had died, and the war claimed many millions more lives across Asia.
復

Occupation, Constitution & Economic Miracle占領・憲法・高度経済成長

The Allied Occupation 占領期1945 – 1952

Under General Douglas MacArthur (SCAP), the overwhelmingly American occupation demilitarized and democratized Japan. It brought land reform that turned tenants into owners, women's suffrage (women first voted in April 1946), legal labor unions, the breakup of the zaibatsu, an educational overhaul, and the separation of State Shinto from government. In his 1 January 1946 rescript the emperor rejected the "false conception" of his divinity. He was kept on the throne and never tried. The Tokyo War Crimes Tribunal (1946–48) convicted 25 leaders and hanged seven, including Tōjō Hideki.

The Constitution of Japan, drafted in English by SCAP staff in February 1946 and revised and adopted by the Diet, was promulgated on 3 November 1946 and took effect on 3 May 1947. It made the people sovereign and the emperor "the symbol of the State and of the unity of the people." Article 9 renounces war "as a sovereign right of the nation." It has never been amended. As the Cold War and the Korean War arrived, the occupation shifted course (the "reverse course"): purges of leftists, economic stabilization, and the creation of what became the Self-Defense Forces (1954). The Treaty of San Francisco (signed 1951, effective 28 April 1952) restored sovereignty, alongside a US–Japan Security Treaty. Okinawa stayed under US administration until 1972.

Later Shōwa: the miracle 高度経済成長1950s – 1980s

The conservative parties merged into the Liberal Democratic Party (LDP) in 1955, beginning the "1955 System" of near-continuous LDP rule. The revised Security Treaty of 1960 provoked the massive Anpo protests. Prime Minister Ikeda Hayato then promised to double incomes in ten years, and the economy did so in about seven. Real growth averaged around 9–10% a year from the mid-1950s to 1973. The 1964 Tokyo Olympics opened alongside the first Shinkansen bullet train. Japan's GNP became the second-largest in the capitalist world in 1968, and the "three sacred treasures" of TV, washing machine and refrigerator filled homes. The costs included grave pollution diseases (Minamata mercury poisoning, Itai-itai disease) and punishing work hours.

Firms like Toyota, Sony, Honda, Panasonic and Nintendo conquered world markets after the 1973 oil shock, aided by lifetime employment, keiretsu networks, quality-control circles and coordination by the Ministry of International Trade and Industry (MITI). After the Plaza Accord (1985) drove up the yen, easy money inflated an asset bubble. At its peak the land under Tokyo's Imperial Palace was rumored to be worth more than all of California, and the Nikkei 225 stock index closed at a record 38,915.87 on 29 December 1989. Emperor Hirohito had died earlier that year, on 7 January 1989, after a 62-year reign.

平

Heisei: Lost Decades & Disasters平成時代

Heisei era 平成8 January 1989 – 30 April 2019 · Emperor Akihito

"Achieving peace" was a hopeful name for an anxious era. The bubble burst in 1990–91: stocks lost more than half their value within a few years, land prices fell for over a decade, and banks hid mountains of bad loans. The 1990s and 2000s became the "Lost Decades", marked by deflation, near-zero growth, "zombie" firms, the rise of insecure non-regular employment, and a generation shut out of the job market (the "employment ice age"). In 2010 China overtook Japan as the world's second-largest economy.

  • 1993: the LDP briefly lost power to an eight-party coalition under Hosokawa Morihiro. The electoral system was reformed in 1994.
  • 1995: the Great Hanshin (Kobe) Earthquake on 17 January killed about 6,400 people. On 20 March the Aum Shinrikyo cult released sarin gas on the Tokyo subway, killing 13 initially and injuring thousands.
  • 1997–98: major financial collapses, including Yamaichi Securities and the Long-Term Credit Bank.
  • 2001–06: the populist reformer Koizumi Jun'ichirō privatized the postal system. His visits to the Yasukuni Shrine strained relations with China and Korea.
  • 2009–12: the Democratic Party of Japan (DPJ) governed after a landslide. It was then swept out after disaster management and policy failures.
  • 2012–20: Abe Shinzō's second term, built on "Abenomics" (aggressive monetary easing, fiscal stimulus and structural reform), the 2015 security laws allowing limited collective self-defense, and diplomacy around the "Free and Open Indo-Pacific". He became Japan's longest-serving prime minister.
3·11: the Great East Japan Earthquake. At 14:46 on 11 March 2011, a magnitude 9.0–9.1 megathrust earthquake off Tōhoku, the most powerful ever recorded in Japan, generated tsunami waves exceeding 30–40 m in places. Nearly 20,000 people died or remain missing (about 15,900 confirmed dead and about 2,500 missing, plus thousands of disaster-related deaths later). At the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear plant, three reactors melted down after the tsunami knocked out cooling, forcing over 150,000 people to evacuate. It was the worst nuclear accident since Chernobyl. Most reactors nationwide were shut down. Treated water releases began in 2023 over objections from China and others, and decommissioning is expected to take decades.

In 2016 Emperor Akihito, who with Empress Michiko had redefined the monarchy through visits to disaster zones and wartime battlefields, signaled a wish to retire. A one-time special law (2017) allowed the first abdication in about two centuries, the last having been Emperor Kōkaku in 1817. Akihito stepped down on 30 April 2019.

Reiwa: Japan Today令和時代（2019年〜）

Reiwa era 令和1 May 2019 – present · Emperor Naruhito

"Reiwa", usually glossed as "beautiful harmony", was the first era name taken from a Japanese classic, a poem preface in the Man'yōshū about plum blossoms, instead of Chinese texts. The era began with festive optimism and was quickly overtaken by the COVID-19 pandemic.

  • Tokyo 2020 Olympics: postponed by a year and held from 23 July to 8 August 2021 with almost no spectators. Japan won a record 27 gold medals.
  • Prime ministers: Abe resigned for health reasons in 2020 and was followed by Suga Yoshihide (2020–21), Kishida Fumio (2021–24) and Ishiba Shigeru (2024–25). In October 2025 Takaichi Sanae became the LDP's leader and Japan's first woman prime minister. She governs with a new partnership with the Japan Innovation Party (Ishin), after Kōmeitō ended its 26-year coalition with the LDP.
  • 8 July 2022: former PM Abe Shinzō was assassinated while campaigning in Nara. The attacker's grievance against the Unification Church set off investigations into the church's ties with politicians and a court order (2025) to dissolve its Japanese branch.
  • Security: the December 2022 strategy documents committed to roughly doubling defense spending toward 2% of GDP by FY2027 and to acquiring "counterstrike" capabilities, a historic shift driven by China, North Korea and Russia's war in Ukraine.
  • Economy: the yen fell to multi-decade lows. The Nikkei finally surpassed its 1989 record in February 2024, and in March 2024 the Bank of Japan ended negative interest rates, its first hike in 17 years. Inflation returned after decades of deflation, along with major wage increases.
  • Disasters: the Noto Peninsula earthquake (M7.5) struck on New Year's Day 2024.
  • Politics: an LDP slush-fund scandal (2023–24) contributed to the ruling coalition losing its lower-house majority in October 2024 and its upper-house majority in July 2025, the most fragile LDP position in decades.
  • Expo 2025 Osaka, Kansai (13 April – 13 October 2025) drew over 25 million visits. Inbound tourism hit records (about 37 million visitors in 2024), bringing worries about "overtourism".
  • Demography: births fell below 700,000 in 2024, the lowest since records began in 1899. About 29% of the population is 65 or older, the highest share of any large country. Foreign residents passed 3.5 million, and immigration policy is being cautiously loosened.

皇

The Imperial Institution & Era Names天皇と元号

Japan's monarchy is often called the oldest continuous hereditary monarchy in the world. By the traditional count, Naruhito is the 126th emperor in a line running back to the mythical Jimmu. Historically verifiable descent reaches at least to the 6th century. For most of that time emperors were priest-kings and patrons of culture, not rulers. Real power belonged to the Soga, the Fujiwara regents, retired emperors, shoguns, and after 1868 to oligarchs and generals acting in the emperor's name. The postwar Constitution makes the emperor a "symbol of the State," with no powers related to government.

Succession is limited to men in the male line by the 1947 Imperial Household Law, although eight women reigned as empress in earlier centuries, two of them twice (Kōgyoku/Saimei and Kōken/Shōtoku). The heirs are the emperor's brother, Crown Prince Fumihito, and his son Prince Hisahito (born 2006). Princesses who marry commoners leave the imperial family, which is shrinking, and the Diet continues to debate reforms such as allowing female members to keep their status. The emperor's only child is Princess Aiko.

Gengō: counting time by eras 元号

Following Chinese practice, Japan began naming eras in 645 with Taika ("Great Change"). Reiwa is the 248th. Before 1868 eras changed frequently, after omens, disasters or the start of a new sixty-year cycle, sometimes several times in one reign. Since Meiji, one era corresponds to one reign (issei ichigen), and emperors are posthumously known by their era name: Hirohito is "Emperor Shōwa." Era names still appear on official documents, coins, and many forms. 2026 is Reiwa 8.

神仏

Shinto, Buddhism & the Sacred神道と仏教

Most Japanese people engage with more than one tradition without seeing a contradiction. A common saying is "born Shinto, married Christian, die Buddhist": newborns are presented at shrines, many weddings are chapel-style, and funerals are overwhelmingly Buddhist. Surveys show that most people do not identify as "religious", yet tens of millions visit shrines and temples at New Year (hatsumōde).

Shinto 神道

"The way of the kami." Kami are sacred powers present in mountains, waterfalls, ancestors, heroes and even scholars: Sugawara no Michizane is worshipped as Tenjin, the god of learning. There is no founder or canonical scripture. The emphasis is on purity (harae), ritual and festival. Shrines are marked by torii gates and shimenawa ropes. Ise Jingū, dedicated to Amaterasu, is rebuilt every 20 years (shikinen sengū; the 62nd rebuilding took place in 2013). Izumo Taisha is where the kami are said to gather each tenth month. From 1868 to 1945 the state turned Shinto into a nationalist ideology ("State Shinto"), which is why the Yasukuni Shrine, where Japan's war dead including convicted war criminals are enshrined, remains politically charged.

Buddhism 仏教

Introduced via Korea in the 6th century. For about a thousand years Buddhism and Shinto were fused (shinbutsu shūgō), with kami understood as local manifestations of buddhas (honji suijaku). The Meiji government forcibly separated them in 1868, and anti-Buddhist violence destroyed many temples.

  • Nara schools (Kegon, Hossō…) and Heian esoteric schools: Tendai and Shingon.
  • Pure Land (Jōdo, Jōdo Shinshū): rebirth in Amida's paradise through faith and the nenbutsu. Today it is the largest family of sects.
  • Zen (Rinzai: kōan; Sōtō: "just sitting", shikantaza; Ōbaku): shaped warrior culture, gardens, ink painting and tea.
  • Nichiren: the Lotus Sutra and the chant Namu Myōhō Renge Kyō, the basis of modern lay movements such as Sōka Gakkai.

Confucianism shaped ethics, government and family life, especially as Neo-Confucian orthodoxy under the Tokugawa. Christianity flourished briefly, was banned and driven underground (the Kakure Kirishitan, or "hidden Christians", whose sites in Nagasaki are now UNESCO-listed), and today counts about 1% of the population. The 19th and 20th centuries produced many shin shūkyō (new religions), such as Tenrikyō and Ōmoto.

Arts, Ways & Traditions芸道と伝統文化

Samurai & bushidō 侍・武士道

The bushi or samurai ("those who serve") arose as provincial mounted archers in the Heian period. They dominated Japan from 1185, became an urban administrative class under the Tokugawa, and were abolished as a legal status in the 1870s. Medieval warrior ideals stressed martial skill, reputation, and reciprocal ties between lord and vassal, and betrayal was common. The systematic code called bushidō is largely a product of Edo-era moralists and of the Meiji period. Works such as Hagakure (1716, "the way of the warrior is found in death") and Nitobe Inazō's English-language Bushido: The Soul of Japan (1900) romanticized loyalty, rectitude and self-sacrifice, and these ideals were later mobilized for militarism. The samurai's material culture endures: the Japanese sword, armor, castles, and martial arts (budō) such as kendō, kyūdō, jūdō (founded by Kanō Jigorō in 1882) and aikidō.

The way of tea 茶道

Tea came with Buddhist monks. Eisai promoted powdered green tea in 1191 and wrote of its health benefits. From competitive tea parties, Murata Jukō, Takeno Jōō and above all Sen no Rikyū developed wabi-cha: rough Raku bowls, tiny huts entered on hands and knees, and the principles of harmony, respect, purity and tranquility (wa kei sei jaku). Rikyū's descendants founded the Omotesenke, Urasenke and Mushakōjisenke schools. The phrase ichi-go ichi-e ("one time, one meeting") captures its ethos.

Ikebana 生け花

Flower arrangement grew out of Buddhist altar offerings. The Ikenobō school, whose priests served at Kyoto's Rokkaku-dō temple, codified the formal rikka style in the 15th–16th centuries. Later styles include the simpler shōka/seika, the casual nageire, and modern schools such as Ohara (moribana) and Sōgetsu (1927, avant-garde). Arrangements emphasize asymmetry, line, empty space and the season.

Theater 能・歌舞伎・文楽

Noh 能

Masked, slow and haunting musical drama perfected by Kan'ami and his son Zeami (c. 1363–1443). Zeami's treatises theorize yūgen and hana, the "flower" of performance. Ghosts, warriors and madwomen appear on a bare cypress stage with a painted pine. Comic kyōgen plays are performed between Noh pieces. It is on UNESCO's list (2008, first proclaimed 2001).

Kabuki 歌舞伎

Begun around 1603 by Izumo no Okuni, a female dancer in Kyoto. Women were banned from the stage in 1629, and then young men, so all-male troupes with onnagata female-role specialists became the norm. Kabuki features flamboyant kumadori makeup, mie poses, the hanamichi walkway into the audience, and revolving stages. Acting dynasties such as Ichikawa Danjūrō continue today.

Bunraku 文楽

Puppet theater (ningyō jōruri), in which three puppeteers in view of the audience operate one large puppet, accompanied by a chanter (tayū) and shamisen. Takemoto Gidayū (from 1684) and the playwright Chikamatsu Monzaemon made it the premier drama of the Genroku era. Many classic kabuki plays began as bunraku. It is based today at the National Bunraku Theatre in Osaka.

Poetry 和歌・俳句

The classical waka, or tanka, has 31 morae (5-7-5-7-7) and was the essential accomplishment of courtiers. Linked verse (renga) became a social art in the Middle Ages, and its comic offshoot haikai gave rise to the independent 17-mora opening verse (hokku). Masaoka Shiki renamed it haiku in the 1890s. The great masters are Bashō (1644–1694), Yosa Buson (1716–1784), Kobayashi Issa (1763–1828) and Shiki. A haiku traditionally contains a seasonal word (kigo) and a "cutting word" (kireji).

Ukiyo-e & painting 浮世絵

Woodblock prints were made by a team: the publisher, the artist who drew the design, the carver, and the printer. They were cheap entertainment that showed courtesans, kabuki stars, sumo wrestlers, landscapes and erotica. After Japan opened, prints flooded Europe, sparking Japonisme: Van Gogh copied Hiroshige, Monet collected prints and built a Japanese bridge at Giverny, and Degas and Toulouse-Lautrec borrowed their flat color fields and bold cropping. Before ukiyo-e came the great schools of Japanese painting: Heian yamato-e narrative scrolls (Genji Monogatari Emaki), Muromachi ink painting, the Kanō school's official style, and the decorative Rinpa school (Sōtatsu, Kōrin). Eccentric Edo masters such as Itō Jakuchū also belong in this story.

Architecture & gardens 建築・庭園

Building in wood

The tradition runs from raised-floor shrines (Ise, Izumo) and continental-style temple halls (Hōryū-ji, Tōdai-ji's Daibutsuden, the largest wooden building in the world for centuries), through the Heian aristocratic shinden-zukuri, warrior-era shoin-zukuri with tatami and tokonoma, and the refined rustic sukiya (Katsura Imperial Villa), to castles such as Himeji, the "White Heron". Joinery without nails, deep eaves, and flexible pagodas that survive earthquakes are hallmarks. Modern Japanese architects such as Tange Kenzō, Andō Tadao, SANAA, Itō Toyoo and Isozaki Arata have made Japan one of the most decorated countries in the history of the Pritzker Prize.

The garden

The Sakuteiki (11th century) is the world's oldest garden manual. Styles include Heian pond gardens imagining Amida's paradise (Byōdō-in); Zen dry landscapes (karesansui) of raked gravel and rock, as at Ryōan-ji and Daisen-in; tea gardens (roji) with stepping stones and lanterns; and Edo stroll gardens with "borrowed scenery" (shakkei). The "Three Great Gardens" are Kenroku-en (Kanazawa), Kōraku-en (Okayama) and Kairaku-en (Mito).

Cuisine 和食

Washoku was added to UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage list in 2013. It is built on rice, seasonal ingredients, fermented foods (miso, soy sauce, sake, natto) and dashi stock from kombu and bonito. In 1908 the chemist Ikeda Kikunae identified the "fifth taste", umami, from kombu. Buddhist influence made eating meat taboo for centuries (the ban was publicly lifted in 1872) and produced shōjin ryōri vegetarian temple cuisine. Kaiseki grew from tea-ceremony meals. Sushi evolved from narezushi, fish fermented in rice, into Edo's fast-food nigiri in the 1820s. Tempura came via the Portuguese, and ramen, curry rice and tonkatsu are modern adaptations of Chinese and Western food. Tokyo has more Michelin-starred restaurants than any other city.

Festivals 祭り

A matsuri is a festival in which the kami are welcomed, entertained and carried through the streets in portable shrines (mikoshi) or on towering floats. Kyoto's Gion Matsuri began in 869 to appease plague spirits and fills all of July. Others include Aomori's illuminated Nebuta floats, Tokushima's Awa Odori dance, Osaka's Tenjin Matsuri boat procession, and Tokyo's Kanda and Sanja festivals. The ritual year includes Shōgatsu (New Year, the most important holiday), Setsubun bean-throwing, hanami (cherry-blossom viewing, tracked nationally by a "sakura front"), Tanabata (the star festival), and Obon in midsummer, when ancestral spirits return and are sent off with lanterns and bon-odori dances.

美

The Vocabulary of Beauty日本の美意識

Japanese aesthetics developed a precise vocabulary for moods that are hard to translate. These concepts overlap and shifted over time, and some were systematized only in the modern era. Even so, they shape everything from poetry to product design.

侘寂

Wabi-sabi

The beauty of the imperfect, impermanent and incomplete. Wabi means rustic simplicity and quiet solitude; sabi is the patina of age. Think of a cracked tea bowl mended with gold (kintsugi) or moss on stone.

物の哀れ

Mono no aware

"The pathos of things": a gentle, bittersweet sensitivity to transience. Motoori Norinaga identified it as the heart of The Tale of Genji. Falling cherry blossoms are its emblem.

間

Ma

Meaningful interval or negative space, in time as well as space: the pause in Noh music, the empty tokonoma alcove, the untouched gravel in a garden, the silence between words.

幽玄

Yūgen

Profound, mysterious grace, suggested rather than shown. It is central to medieval poetics and Zeami's Noh: "to watch the sun sink behind a flower-clad hill… to wander in a huge forest without thought of return."

粋

Iki

Edo townsfolk's urbane chic: understated, worldly, a little flirtatious, never trying too hard. The philosopher Kuki Shūzō analyzed it in The Structure of Iki (1930). Think of the plain kimono with a gorgeous lining.

渋い

Shibui

Subtle, unobtrusive beauty that deepens with time. It describes a quietly perfect object, often in muted tones, and was championed by the folk-craft (mingei) movement of Yanagi Sōetsu.

漫

Modern Pop Cultureポップカルチャー

Since the late 20th century, Japan's most influential export may be its imagination. Precursors include Hokusai's sketchbooks, the "Hokusai Manga" of 1814, and 12th-century comic scrolls of frolicking animals (Chōjū-giga).

Manga & anime

Tezuka Osamu, the "god of manga", pioneered cinematic storytelling (Astro Boy, manga from 1952 and TV anime from 1963). Weekly magazines like Shōnen Jump launched global franchises such as Dragon Ball, One Piece (one of the best-selling comics in history) and Naruto. Studio Ghibli (founded 1985) with Miyazaki Hayao won Oscars for Spirited Away (2003) and The Boy and the Heron (2024). Akira, Ghost in the Shell and Evangelion shaped science fiction worldwide, and Demon Slayer: Mugen Train (2020) became Japan's highest-grossing film.

Games & gadgets

Nintendo (founded 1889 as a playing-card maker), Sega, Sony's PlayStation (1994), Square Enix, Capcom and others defined video gaming. Landmarks include Space Invaders (1978), Pac-Man (1980), Super Mario Bros. (1985), The Legend of Zelda, Final Fantasy and Pokémon (1996), the world's highest-grossing media franchise. Sony's Walkman (1979), karaoke, emoji (from the Japanese for "picture character") and the culture of kawaii (Hello Kitty, 1974) all traveled widely.

Film

The golden age of Japanese cinema produced Kurosawa Akira (Rashōmon, 1950; Seven Samurai, 1954), Ozu Yasujirō (Tokyo Story) and Mizoguchi Kenji. Godzilla (1954) was born from nuclear trauma, and Godzilla Minus One won the Oscar for visual effects in 2024. Later filmmakers include Kitano Takeshi, Koreeda Hirokazu (Palme d'Or, Shoplifters, 2018) and Hamaguchi Ryūsuke (Drive My Car, Oscar 2022).

Music & more

Postwar kayōkyoku and enka led to the 1980s city pop revival, Yellow Magic Orchestra's synth-pop, J-pop megastars (Utada Hikaru, Hamasaki Ayumi), idol groups (AKB48), and visual-kei and J-rock. Vocaloid's Hatsune Miku (2007) became a virtual idol. Recent global streaming hits include YOASOBI and Creepy Nuts. Literature reaches world readers through Kawabata Yasunari and Ōe Kenzaburō (both Nobel laureates), Mishima Yukio and Murakami Haruki.

語

Language & the Writing System日本語と文字

Origins 日本語の起源

Japanese belongs to the small Japonic family together with the Ryukyuan languages. Its deeper origins are one of linguistics' great puzzles. The once-popular "Altaic" hypothesis (linking it with Korean, Mongolic, Turkic and Tungusic) is now widely rejected. A relationship with Korean is plausible but unproven, since shared features may come from long contact. Others propose an Austronesian substrate. Place names recorded in the Korean Samguk sagi suggest that "Peninsular Japonic" languages were once spoken in Korea. A common view is that proto-Japonic arrived with Yayoi rice farmers from the peninsula around the first millennium BCE, gradually replacing Jōmon languages, of which the Ainu language may be a distant relative or simply a neighbor.

Old Japanese, the language of the 8th-century Man'yōshū and Kojiki, distinguished more vowel qualities than today's five (the kō-otsu distinction recorded in man'yōgana spellings). Japanese is agglutinative, subject–object–verb, and topic-prominent (marked with は wa), freely drops pronouns, and uses a pitch accent. Roughly half of its vocabulary consists of Sino-Japanese words (kango), alongside native wago and a growing number of loanwords (gairaigo), mostly from English.

  • Each kanji can have several readings: on'yomi (Sino-Japanese, which can come from different Chinese eras: Go-on, Kan-on, Tō-on) and native kun'yomi. For example, 山 is read san (as in Fuji-san) or yama.
  • The Iroha poem (c. 11th c.) uses every kana exactly once and served as the "alphabet" order. The modern gojūon (a, i, u, e, o / ka, ki…) table is based on Sanskrit phonetics.
  • 1900: hiragana standardized to one form per sound; the variant forms (hentaigana) fell out of use.
  • The genbun itchi movement (late 19th to early 20th c.) unified the written and spoken language. Novelists such as Futabatei Shimei led the way.
  • 1946: postwar reforms introduced the Tōyō kanji list (1,850 characters), modern kana spelling and simplified forms (shinjitai). Some reformers had even proposed abolishing kanji. The list was replaced by the Jōyō kanji in 1981 (1,945 characters), expanded in 2010 to 2,136.
  • Rōmaji: the Hepburn (1867) and Kunrei romanization systems compete. In 2025 a government advisory council recommended making Hepburn-style spellings the official standard.

Keigo: the grammar of respect 敬語

Japanese builds social relationships into grammar. Speakers choose forms according to the listener's status and to the line between in-group and out-group (uchi/soto). For example, when speaking to a client you humble your own boss.

Teineigo 丁寧語

Polite: -desu/-masu forms.
行きます ikimasu, "I/they go".

Sonkeigo 尊敬語

Honorific: elevates the other person's actions.
いらっしゃいます irasshaimasu, "(you, respected) go".

Kenjōgo 謙譲語

Humble: lowers one's own actions.
参ります mairimasu, "I (humbly) go".

Name suffixes add another layer: -san (neutral polite), -sama (esteemed), -kun (junior males), -chan (affectionate), and -sensei (teachers, doctors, artists).

Dialects 方言

Standard Japanese (hyōjungo) is based on educated Tokyo speech and spread through schools and broadcasting. Regional dialects, though, remain vivid: Kansai-ben (Osaka/Kyoto: ōkini for "thanks", -hen negatives) is beloved in comedy. Tōhoku dialects can be hard for outsiders to follow, and Kagoshima speech was reportedly used as a code during WWII. Some scholars treat Hachijō, spoken on Hachijō Island, as a separate language preserving features of eastern Old Japanese.

民

Ainu & Ryukyuan Peoplesアイヌと琉球

The idea of Japan as ethnically homogeneous, which became prominent in postwar rhetoric, overlooks Indigenous and minority peoples, as well as the Zainichi Korean community and a growing number of immigrants.

The Ainu アイヌ

The Indigenous people of Hokkaidō, southern Sakhalin and the Kurils, descended from Jōmon peoples via the Satsumon and Okhotsk cultures. Their culture centered on salmon, deer, and a rich spiritual world of kamuy, including the bear ceremony (iyomante), and the oral epics (yukar). Trade with Japanese, monopolized after 1604 by the Matsumae domain, became exploitative. Revolts such as Shakushain's War (1669) were crushed. After 1869 Meiji colonization took land, banned customs such as tattoos and earrings, and imposed assimilation. The Ainu language is an isolate and critically endangered, with very few native speakers left, though revival efforts are active. The Diet recognized the Ainu as Indigenous in 2008, the Ainu Promotion Act followed in 2019, and the national museum Upopoy opened in 2020.

Ryūkyū / Okinawa 琉球

The southern islands developed their own gusuku (castle) culture. In 1429 Shō Hashi unified the Ryūkyū Kingdom, a maritime trading state paying tribute to Ming China. Its capital was Shuri Castle, which burned in 2019 and is due to be rebuilt by 2026. Invaded by Satsuma in 1609, the kingdom was quietly subordinated while still sending tribute to China, then annexed in 1879. The Ryukyuan languages (Amami, Kunigami, Okinawan, Miyako, Yaeyama, Yonaguni) are Japonic but not mutually intelligible with Japanese, and UNESCO lists all of them as endangered. Okinawa suffered the bloodiest land battle of the Pacific War, remained under US rule until 1972, and still hosts about 70% of the land area exclusively used by US bases in Japan, a constant political grievance. Its music (sanshin), karate, which originated there, Bingata dyeing and famous longevity are distinctive.
