01 · How Japanese is written 書き方

Three scripts, one sentence.

Japanese mixes three writing systems in everyday text — plus romaji, the Latin-alphabet spelling learners use as training wheels. They each have a job.

46 basic
ひらがな

Hiragana

The curvy phonetic script. Writes grammar — particles, verb endings (okurigana) — and native words without (or with hard) kanji. Each symbol is one sound unit, a mora.

46 basic
カタカナ

Katakana

The angular twin. Same sounds, used for foreign loanwords (コーヒー kōhī, coffee), foreign names, onomatopoeia, and emphasis — like italics.

~2,136 jōyō
漢字

Kanji

Characters borrowed from Chinese that carry meaning. They write the cores of nouns, verbs and adjectives. Most have several readings: on'yomi (Chinese-derived) and kun'yomi (native).

training wheels
rōmaji

Romaji

Japanese in Latin letters (this site uses Hepburn: shi, chi, tsu, fu; macrons for long vowels like ō). Handy at first — but drop it as soon as kana click.

Why learn kana first?

  • They're finite. 46 + 46 basic shapes, learnable in 1–3 weeks with mnemonics.
  • Romaji misleads. Spellings like “ou”, “ei” and “n” hide real pronunciation; kana show it directly.
  • Every resource assumes them. Textbooks, dictionaries and furigana (kana over kanji) all need kana.
  • Grammar lives in hiragana. Particles and verb endings are always kana.

Your first-month roadmap

Week 1 Hiragana with mnemonics, 10–15 per day, quiz daily.
Week 2 Katakana + dakuten/yōon; read your name and menu words.
Week 3 Sounds, です/ます sentences, the core particles.
Week 4 First 50 kanji, numbers, survival phrases; start Anki.
02 · The kana 五十音

The gojūon chart, with a story for every sound.

Tap a card to flip it, hear it, and see its mnemonic in the spotlight. Read the chart by rows: consonant + vowel (k + a = ka). Toggle scripts — the sounds stay the same.

Gaps are historical: yi, ye, wi, wu, we aren't used in modern Japanese. は is read wa and へ e when used as particles; を (wo) is pronounced o.

Dakuten ゛ & handakuten ゜

Two little marks change the consonant: the “ten-ten” voices it (k→g, s→z, t→d, h→b); the “maru” circle turns h into p.

ぢ/づ sound like じ/ず (ji/zu) and are rare — mostly in compounds like はなぢ (nosebleed) and つづく (to continue).

Yōon combinations ゃゅょ

An i-column kana + a small や/ゆ/よ merge into one beat: き + ゃ = きゃ kya. Size matters: きや is ki-ya, two beats.

04 · Where kana came from 字源

Every kana was once a kanji.

Around the 9th century, scribes used Chinese characters purely for their sounds (man'yōgana). Hiragana grew from cursive simplifications of whole characters; katakana from fragments — often just one side — used by monks to annotate texts.

e.g. 安 → あ (cursive) · 阿 → ア (left part ⻖)

The source of ン is debated (one theory is 尓; another is an old phonetic mark). 祢 is the variant of 禰.

07 · Pronunciation 発音

Five vowels. Steady rhythm. Gentle melody.

Japanese has only five pure vowels, and every mora gets about the same length — like a metronome. Get these right and you'll sound clearer than most learners.

Long vowels

Hold the vowel for two beats. In hiragana, add a vowel (おばあさん); in katakana, use the bar ー. Length changes meaning!

Small っ — the pause

A small っ/ッ doubles the next consonant: a one-beat silent “hold” before it, like the gap in “bookcase”.

ん and silent vowels

ん is a full beat of its own: こんにちは = ko-n-ni-chi-wa (5 beats). In standard speech, i and u often whisper between voiceless consonants: です ≈ “dess”, します ≈ “shee-mass”.

Pitch accent — a gentle intro

Japanese uses high/low pitch rather than English-style stress. Standard (Tokyo) Japanese can distinguish words purely by pitch. Don't panic: context usually saves you, but listening for it makes you sound natural. Pink dots are high, the hollow dot shows the pitch on a following particle (が).

Synthesized voices may not render pitch perfectly — for real study, try the OJAD pitch dictionary or Dogen's pitch-accent course.

08 · Grammar primer 文法

Verb last. Particles do the heavy lifting.

Japanese is SOV: Subject–Object–Verb. Little particles after each word mark its role, so word order is flexible — except that the verb comes at the end.

Word order: English vs Japanese

私はコーヒーを飲みます。Watashi wa kōhī o nomimasu. — “I drink coffee.” In practice, 私は is often dropped when obvious: コーヒーを飲みます。

Three verb groups

Ru-verbs (ichidan): end in -iru/-eru; drop る and add endings. U-verbs (godan): the final u-sound shifts. Irregular: just する and 来る. Some -eru/-iru words are u-verbs (帰る kaeru, 切る kiru “cut”, 入る hairu).

い-adjectives vs な-adjectives

い-adjectives end in い and conjugate themselves. な-adjectives behave like nouns: they take な before a noun and use です/だ. Trap: きれい (pretty) and きらい (disliked) look like い-adjectives but are な-adjectives. いい (good) conjugates from よい.

09 · Numbers & counters 数字

Count like a local.

Japanese numbers are wonderfully regular: 11 is 十一 “ten-one”, 25 is 二十五 “two-ten-five”. Click a number to hear it.

Big numbers: think in 万

Japanese groups by 10,000 (万 man), not 1,000. So 100,000 = 十万 (jūman, “ten man”), and one million = 百万 (hyakuman).

Counters basics

You can't just say “three cats”: you attach a counter that fits the object's shape or category. Sounds often shift (rendaku/gemination) — memorize the common ones.

10 · First kanji 漢字

58 starter kanji, built from pictures.

Many first kanji are pictographs (山 looks like a mountain), and many more are combinations of simpler parts called radicals (休 = person 亻 + tree 木 → resting). Learn the parts and the rest snap together.

Readings: カタカナ = on'yomi (Chinese-derived, used in compounds), ひらがな = kun'yomi (native Japanese, used alone or with okurigana in parentheses).

12 · Politeness levels 敬語

Keigo: climbing the ladder of respect.

Japanese changes verbs and vocabulary depending on who you're talking to and about. You'll start with polite です/ます; recognize the others first, produce them later.

Casual食べる普通体 · friends, family, inner thoughts
Polite · 丁寧語食べますteineigo · strangers, coworkers — your default
Respectful · 尊敬語召し上がるsonkeigo · elevates others' actions (customers, bosses)
Humble · 謙譲語いただくkenjōgo · lowers your own actions toward others

You'll hear keigo constantly as a customer: いらっしゃいませ (“welcome”) in every shop is sonkeigo. Beautifying prefixes お/ご (お水, ご飯) are another everyday layer, sometimes called 美化語.
