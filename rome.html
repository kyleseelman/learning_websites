Liber I · 753–509 BC

A wolf, two twins, and seven hills

Romans dated their city from 21 April 753 BC, a date calculated in the 1st century BC by the scholar Varro. The story they told was one of gods, a she-wolf, and fratricide. What archaeologists find is humbler and just as interesting.

The Seven Hills

Hover a hill

Rome grew at the lowest easy crossing of the Tiber, where an island split the river, and where the salt route from the coast (the later Via Salaria) met the north–south routes of central Italy.

The Seven Kings of Rome

Traditional dates. The kings are semi-legendary, but the last three show strong Etruscan connections that archaeology supports.

Liber II · 509–27 BC

Res publica: the public thing

After expelling Tarquin the Proud, the Romans built a system designed so that no single man could ever be king again. Power was split, shared, time-limited, and vetoable. Click any part of the constitution to see how it worked.

The Cursus Honorum

The "course of honours": the ladder of offices for an ambitious Roman. Minimum ages follow Sulla's reforms (81 BC), building on the Lex Villia Annalis of 180 BC.

The Struggle of the Orders

For two centuries the plebeians (the common citizens) fought the patrician aristocracy for political equality, often by simply walking out of the city (a secessio).

494 BC

First Secession

Plebeians withdraw to the Sacred Mount. They win their own officers, the tribunes of the plebs, whose persons are sacrosanct.

451–450 BC

The Twelve Tables

Rome's law is written down and publicly displayed for the first time, so patrician judges can no longer interpret secret custom at will.

445 BC

Lex Canuleia

Marriage between patricians and plebeians is legalised.

367 BC

Licinian–Sextian laws

One consulship opened to plebeians (traditionally; first plebeian consul 366 BC).

287 BC

Lex Hortensia

Resolutions of the plebeian assembly (plebiscita) become binding on all Romans. The struggle formally ends; a mixed patrician–plebeian "nobility" now rules.

Liber III · 509–50 BC

From Latium to Mare Nostrum

Rome did not set out with a master plan for world rule. It fought neighbour after neighbour and turned defeated enemies into allies who owed it soldiers. That bottomless reserve of manpower let Rome lose battles, sometimes catastrophically, and still win wars.

396 BC

Veii falls

After a traditional ten-year siege Rome takes its great Etruscan rival 16 km to the north and doubles its territory.

c. 390 BC

The Gallic sack

Gauls under Brennus rout a Roman army at the Allia and sack the city (Polybius dates it to 387/386). Rome later builds the "Servian" wall from Grotta Oscura tufa.

343–290 BC

Samnite Wars

Three wars against the tough hill peoples of the Apennines. Humiliated at the Caudine Forks (321), Rome wins at Sentinum (295).

280–275 BC

Pyrrhus of Epirus

The Greek king wins at Heraclea and Asculum at such cost that a "Pyrrhic victory" becomes proverbial. By 272 Tarentum falls; Rome rules peninsular Italy.

The Punic Wars: Rome vs Carthage

First · 264–241 BC

A war for Sicily

Rome builds its first serious war fleet, reportedly copying a stranded Carthaginian ship, and fits it with the corvus boarding bridge. It wins at Mylae (260) and the Aegates Islands (241). Sicily becomes the first province.

Second · 218–201 BC

Hannibal

Hannibal marches from Spain over the Alps with tens of thousands of men and 37 elephants, and destroys Roman armies at the Trebia, Lake Trasimene and Cannae. Rome refuses to make peace, takes Spain, and Scipio beats Hannibal at Zama (202).

Third · 149–146 BC

Carthago delenda est

Cato the Elder reportedly ended his speeches insisting Carthage must be destroyed. After a three-year siege the city is razed in 146 BC and its territory becomes the province of Africa. Corinth is destroyed the same year.

East and West

200–146 BC

The Hellenistic East

Rome defeats Macedon at Cynoscephalae (197) and Pydna (168), and the Seleucid king Antiochus III at Magnesia (190). In 146 Macedonia becomes a province and Corinth is sacked.

133 BC

A kingdom by will

Attalus III of Pergamum bequeaths his kingdom to Rome; it becomes the rich province of Asia. Scipio Aemilianus takes Numantia in Spain the same year.

58–50 BC

Caesar in Gaul

Caesar conquers Gaul in eight years, bridges the Rhine, twice lands in Britain, and crushes Vercingetorix at Alesia (52). Ancient tallies of the Gallic dead run to a million, a figure modern historians treat with caution.

Liber IV · 133–27 BC

The Republic devours itself

Conquest brought slaves, silver and vast estates to the rich, and debt and dispossession to many small farmers who made up the legions. A century of reformers, generals and street violence ended with one man ruling the Roman world.

10 January 49 BC

Crossing the Rubicon

A general who led an army across this small stream from his province of Cisalpine Gaul into Italy committed treason. Caesar's command was ending, and his enemies in the Senate meant to prosecute him.

Liber V · 27 BC – AD 235

Augustus and the Caesars

In 27 BC the Senate gave Octavian the name Augustus. He claimed to have restored the Republic while holding the real levers of power: the army, the treasury and the tribunician power. The regime he built, the Principate, ran for over two centuries.

"I found Rome a city of brick and left it a city of marble."Augustus, as reported by Suetonius (Augustus 28). Cassius Dio says he meant the strength of the empire.
27 BC – AD 180

Pax Romana

About two centuries of relative internal peace and prosperity across the Mediterranean, although frontier wars, revolts (Judaea, Boudica) and civil war in AD 69 still happened.

AD 212

Everyone a citizen

Caracalla's Constitutio Antoniniana grants citizenship to nearly all free inhabitants of the empire. Cassius Dio claimed the motive was to raise more tax.

Liber VI · AD 117

The empire at its greatest extent

When Trajan died in August 117, Roman power stretched from the Atlantic to the Persian Gulf and from Scotland's border to the Sahara: about 5 million km². His successor Hadrian promptly gave up the new conquests in Mesopotamia and Armenia. Hover over a province.

Gladius

A short stabbing sword, probably adapted from Iberian designs. Roman soldiers were trained to thrust rather than slash.

Pilum

A heavy javelin with a long iron shank that bent or stuck in shields, leaving them useless. Two were thrown just before contact.

Scutum

A large curved shield of laminated wood, about 1.2 m tall, used both as a wall and as a weapon (the boss could punch).

Pay & service

225 denarii a year under Augustus, raised to 300 by Domitian. Veterans received land or a cash bounty on discharge.

Liber VIII · Opera publica

Roads, water and concrete

Rome built more than 400,000 km of roads, over 80,000 km of them paved with stone. Pick two cities and the planner finds a route over the main network, then estimates how long the journey took.

Itinerarium · route planner

Or click two cities on the map. Speeds: foot ≈30 km/day, ox cart ≈15, courier of the cursus publicus ≈75, merchant ship ≈110 km/day in good weather. Real trips depended on season, wind and terrain (compare Stanford's ORBIS model).

Aqueduct cross-section

Gravity, and patience

Aqueducts carried water entirely by gravity, mostly in buried channels; the famous arches account for only a small part of their length. The aqueduct that crosses the Pont du Gard falls only about 12.6 m over its ≈50 km course, an average of roughly 25 cm per km.

Too shallow and the water stagnates and silts up; too steep and it scours the channel. Surveyors used the chorobates (a long levelling table) and the groma to hold gradients this gentle.

Rome itself was fed by 11 aqueducts; estimates of their total daily flow vary widely, from about 0.5 to 1 million m³.

The Pantheon

Rebuilt under Hadrian (c. AD 113–125). Its 43.3 m dome is still the largest unreinforced concrete dome in the world. The height to the oculus equals the diameter, so a perfect sphere fits inside. The concrete gets lighter toward the top, with pumice in the upper layers.

The Colosseum: who sat where

The Flavian Amphitheatre, inaugurated by Titus in AD 80 with 100 days of games. It measures about 189 × 156 m and held perhaps 50,000 spectators (estimates range up to 80,000). Click a seating zone.

Opus caementicium

Roman concrete mixed lime with volcanic ash (pulvis puteolanus, pozzolana). It set even underwater, which made harbours like Caesarea's possible. Recent research suggests unmixed lime clasts let it partly heal its own cracks.

The arch & vault

Romans did not invent the arch, but they used arches, barrel vaults and domes on a scale no earlier civilisation had, enclosing huge interior spaces like the Baths of Caracalla.

Cloaca Maxima

The "Greatest Sewer", traditionally begun under the Tarquin kings to drain the Forum valley, still carries water to the Tiber.

Liber IX · Vita Romana

Bread, circuses, and silver

Imperial Rome held perhaps a million people, more than any European city again until 19th-century London. Explore how they lived, what they believed, and what happened to their money.

Economy · the debasement of the denarius

When money stops being money

The silver denarius began near-pure. Emperors who needed to pay armies quietly reduced its silver and weight. Nero cut it in AD 64; under the Severans it fell to about half silver; by the 260s the "silver" coin (now the antoninianus) was bronze with a thin silver wash.

Values are approximate averages from metallurgical studies and vary between issues and mints. From 215 the main silver coin was the antoninianus, nominally worth two denarii.

The gods of Rome

Click a card to turn it over

Roman religion was a matter of public ritual and contract (do ut des, "I give so that you may give") more than belief. Many deities were identified with Greek counterparts. Mystery cults such as those of Isis and Mithras, the imperial cult, and eventually Christianity all spread through the empire.

Liber X · AD 235–284

Fifty years, dozens of emperors

When soldiers murdered Severus Alexander in 235, they opened half a century of near-constant civil war, invasion, plague and inflation. Over twenty men were recognised as emperor, not counting a swarm of usurpers. Most were killed by their own troops.

Liber XI · AD 284–395

Four rulers, one new faith

Diocletian (284–305) decided the empire was too big for one man. In 293 he set up the Tetrarchy: two senior emperors (Augusti) and two junior (Caesares), each with a court and army close to a threatened frontier. It outlived him by barely a year. Out of the civil wars that followed came Constantine, and with him a Christian empire.

A Christian state

Theodosius I's Edict of Thessalonica makes Nicene Christianity the state religion; pagan sacrifice is banned in 391–392.

How fast did Christianity grow?

One influential model (sociologist Rodney Stark, 1996) assumes steady growth of about 40% per decade from roughly 1,000 believers in AD 40, reaching ≈6 million (≈10%) by 300 and over 30 million, a majority, by 350. These are model outputs, not counts, and are debated.

Liber XIII · AD 395–1453

The Rome that didn't fall

Its citizens never called it "Byzantine", a label popularised by the 16th-century scholar Hieronymus Wolf. They called themselves Rhomaioi, Romans. For nearly a thousand years after 476 the eastern empire, ruled from Constantinople and Greek-speaking, carried the name of Rome.

Liber XIV · Hereditas

Rome is still all around you

The alphabet you are reading, the calendar on your wall, the word "senate", the dome over a capitol building, the civil law codes of much of the world: Rome left its mark on almost everything.

⚖ Law

Justinian's Corpus Juris Civilis (529–534), rediscovered in 11th-century Italy, underlies the civil law systems of continental Europe, Latin America and beyond.

🗣 Language

Latin evolved into Italian, Spanish, Portuguese, French and Romanian, the Romance languages, now spoken by hundreds of millions. A large share of English vocabulary comes from Latin, directly or through French.

📅 Calendar

Caesar's Julian calendar (from 45 BC) gave us 365¼ days and months named for Janus, Mars, Julius and Augustus. Pope Gregory XIII adjusted it in 1582.

🏛 Government

Republic, senate, veto ("I forbid"), census, dictator, consul, and the idea of checks between offices were studied closely by the framers of modern republics.

⛪ The Church

The Roman Church kept Latin, the provincial structure (dioceses) and the title Pontifex Maximus; Eastern Orthodoxy grew from Constantinople.

🏙 Cities

London, Paris, Cologne, Vienna, Barcelona, York and many more grew from Roman foundations, and many modern roads still follow Roman routes.

🔤 Alphabet

The Latin script is the most widely used writing system on Earth.

🏗 Architecture

Domes, arches, basilicas and columns run from the Renaissance to the US Capitol and neoclassical banks.

Notes on sources & simplifications

Coastlines, rivers and borders are hand-drawn approximations on an equirectangular projection (about 11°W–48°E, 24°–57°N). Territory is shown as regional blocks that change hands at representative dates; client kingdoms, frontier zones and contested areas are simplified, and some transfers (for example the post-476 kingdoms) took place over years rather than at a single date.

Ancient numbers (army sizes, casualties, populations) are uncertain, and the figures shown here are commonly cited modern estimates. Dates for the regal period and early Republic follow Roman tradition (Livy, Dionysius, Varro) and are partly legendary. Quotations are limited to well-attested ancient sources, with the source named.

Standard references include Mary Beard, SPQR; Edward Gibbon, The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire; Peter Heather, The Fall of the Roman Empire; Bryan Ward-Perkins, The Fall of Rome and the End of Civilization; Kyle Harper, The Fate of Rome; Peter Brown, The World of Late Antiquity; Adrian Goldsworthy, How Rome Fell; Walter Scheidel's demographic work; and Stanford's ORBIS project.
