Ancient Greece

From the palaces of Bronze Age Crete to Alexander’s empire and the Library of Alexandria, plus an epilogue that follows the Greeks to the present day.

Prologue

A Civilization of Islands and Arguments

There was no single country called “Greece” in antiquity. There were hundreds of fiercely independent communities that shared a language, a set of gods and an argumentative habit of mind.

When we say “Ancient Greece” we mean a world rather than a state. Its people called themselves Hellenes and their world Hellas. The Romans called them Graeci, which is where our word comes from. At its height the Greek world stretched from the coast of Spain to the Crimea and from the Nile delta to the foothills of the Alps. After Alexander it reached Afghanistan and the Punjab. For most of its history it was politically fragmented into somewhere above a thousand poleis, or city-states. The Copenhagen Polis Centre’s inventory catalogues 1,035 of them for the Archaic and Classical periods. Some were great cities. Most were villages with a wall, an assembly place and a temple.

What united them was culture rather than government. They shared the Greek language in its many dialects (Ionic, Doric, Aeolic, Attic). They worshipped the same Olympian gods, recited the epics of Homer and competed at the same Panhellenic games. They believed themselves distinct from the barbaroi, people whose speech sounded like “bar-bar.” Herodotus summed up this shared identity as “the same blood, the same language, the shared shrines and sacrifices of the gods, and the same customs” (Histories 8.144).

This page follows that world from the first farmers of the Neolithic through the Bronze Age palaces of Crete and Mycenae, the long Dark Age, the explosive creativity of the Archaic and Classical eras, the conquests of Alexander and the cosmopolitan Hellenistic kingdoms, to the Roman conquest. An epilogue then follows Greek history forward through Byzantium, the Ottoman centuries, the War of Independence and the modern Hellenic Republic. Along the way we look at how people lived, what they believed and what they built, and why they still matter.

A note on dates and evidence

Dates before about 600 BCE are approximate, and Bronze Age dates are still debated by archaeologists. Ancient population figures are modern estimates with wide error margins. Where the sources disagree, or where legend shades into history, this page says so. “c.” means circa (approximately), and Athenian years often appear as “508/7” because the Athenian civic year began in midsummer.

Β΄

Geography

The Wine-Dark Sea

Mountains divided the Greeks and the sea connected them. Much of Greek history follows from that one geographical fact.

Mainland Greece is a crumpled, rocky peninsula at the southern tip of the Balkans. Roughly four-fifths of it is mountainous. The Pindus range runs down its spine, and spurs like Parnassus, Helicon, Taygetus and Olympus (at 2,918 m the highest peak, and home of the gods) cut the land into small, isolated plains: Attica, Boeotia, the Argolid, Laconia, Messenia, Thessaly. Each plain could support one community, and each community jealously guarded its independence. Geography did not cause the polis, but it made a unified Greek state very difficult to build.

The sea pulled the other way. No point in Greece is much more than 90 km from salt water. The Aegean is studded with islands (the Cyclades, the Dodecanese, the Sporades, great Euboea, Crete) set close enough that a sailor was rarely out of sight of land. Homer’s “wine-dark sea” (οἶνοψ πόντος) was a highway. Grain, timber, metals, pottery, slaves, ideas, alphabets and gods all travelled across it. Seasonal winds shaped the sailing calendar. The summer meltemi (the ancient Etesian winds) blows hard from the north, and most voyaging stopped in winter.

The climate is Mediterranean: hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters. It supports the “Mediterranean triad” of grain (mostly barley, which tolerates thin soils better than wheat), olives and vines. Good farmland was scarce. Attica could not feed its population by the fifth century and depended on grain from the Black Sea, Egypt and Sicily, so control of the sea lanes became a matter of survival. Land hunger also helps explain the great colonizing movement of the 8th–6th centuries BCE, which planted Greek cities from Marseille to the Crimea. Plato has Socrates say that the Greeks live around the sea “like ants or frogs around a pond” (Phaedo 109b).

Prehistory · to c. 3200 BCE

Before History: Hunters, Farmers and Marble Idols

Long before Homer, people were sailing to Melos for obsidian and carving folded-arm figures from Cycladic marble.

Humans and their ancestors have lived in Greece for a very long time. The Petralona skull from Chalkidiki is a pre-modern human perhaps 200,000–300,000 years old. A fragmentary skull from Apidima cave in the Mani was argued in 2019 to belong to an early Homo sapiens about 210,000 years old, a claim that is still debated. At Franchthi Cave in the Argolid, people lived almost continuously from about 40,000 years ago into the Neolithic. One of the most remarkable finds there is obsidian, volcanic glass for blades, brought from the island of Melos by about 11,000 BCE. That means people were crossing open water in the Aegean thousands of years before farming.

The Neolithic revolution (c. 7000–3200 BCE)

Farming reached Greece from Anatolia and the Near East around 7000–6500 BCE, together with domesticated wheat, barley, lentils, sheep and goats. The fertile plains of Thessaly and Macedonia filled with settlement mounds, called magoules. The best-known sites are Sesklo (a large village with fine painted pottery, at its height c. 6000–5500 BCE) and Dimini (c. 4800–4500 BCE, with concentric stone enclosures). Knossos on Crete was first settled in the early Neolithic, which makes it one of the oldest continuously occupied places in Europe. Neolithic Greeks made figurines, many of them female, whose meaning is still disputed, and by the later Neolithic they had begun working copper.

The Early Bronze Age and the Cycladic culture (c. 3200–2000 BCE)

Around 3200 BCE bronze-working (copper alloyed with arsenic, and later with tin) spread through the Aegean, and with it long-distance trade. The three regions of the Aegean Bronze Age take their names from geography: Helladic (mainland), Cycladic (islands) and Minoan (Crete). On the mainland, places like Lerna in the Argolid built monumental “corridor houses” such as the House of Tiles.

The Cycladic islanders left the period’s most haunting art. Their marble figurines, mostly nude women with arms folded across the stomach, faces reduced to a nose and a tilted oval, range from a few centimetres to nearly life-size. Their stark abstraction later fascinated modern artists such as Brâncuși, Modigliani and Henry Moore. They were originally painted, with eyes, hair, jewellery and tattoo-like patterns that are now mostly lost. Many were found in graves, but their purpose remains unknown. Sadly, the market for “Cycladic art” in the twentieth century fuelled massive looting, so most surviving examples have lost their archaeological context.

Ε΄

Aegean Bronze Age · c. 2000–1100 BCE

Palaces of Bulls and Lords of Gold

Two brilliant palace civilizations, the Minoans of Crete and the Mycenaeans of the mainland, then a catastrophe that ended them both.

Minoan Crete

“Minoan” is a modern label. Sir Arthur Evans, who excavated Knossos from 1900, named the culture after the legendary King Minos. We do not know what the Minoans called themselves. Around 1900 BCE (the “Protopalatial” period) great palace complexes rose at Knossos, Phaistos, Malia and later Zakros. They were rebuilt even more grandly after destructions around 1700 BCE, in the “Neopalatial” period. Knossos covered some 20,000 m², with multi-storey wings, light wells, drainage, magazines full of storage jars (pithoi) and vivid frescoes of lilies, dolphins, processions and the famous bull-leaping. The palaces seem to have been centres of religion, administration, craft production and redistribution all at once.

The Minoans were a maritime power with contacts in Egypt (Minoan-style frescoes have been found at Tell el-Dab’a in the Nile Delta), the Levant and the Cyclades. They wrote in two scripts, Cretan hieroglyphic and Linear A, which remain undeciphered. We can read the numbers and some signs (borrowed later into Linear B), but the language is unknown and does not appear to be Greek. The Phaistos Disc, a clay disc stamped with a unique spiral of signs, is equally mysterious. Strikingly, Minoan palaces lack obvious fortifications, and their art shows few images of war. Older writers took this as proof of a peaceful “Pax Minoica.” Most scholars now doubt anything so idyllic.

The Thera eruption

In the late 17th or early 16th century BCE, the volcanic island of Thera (Santorini) exploded in one of the largest eruptions of the past several thousand years. Radiocarbon dating points to about 1600 BCE or a little earlier, while traditional archaeological chronologies favoured about 1500 BCE, and the debate continues. The Bronze Age town of Akrotiri was buried under metres of ash. Its inhabitants seem to have evacuated, because very few bodies have been found. Akrotiri preserved multi-storey houses and superb frescoes, including the Spring fresco with swallows and the Flotilla fresco. The eruption brought ash fall and tsunamis to Crete, but Minoan palaces carried on for several generations afterward, so it was not the single blow that ended the Minoans. Some have linked the disaster to Plato’s tale of Atlantis, an intriguing but unprovable idea.

Around 1450 BCE most Cretan palaces were destroyed, and only Knossos survived, now apparently under Mycenaean control: its later archives are written in Greek Linear B. Myths of Theseus and the Minotaur, the labyrinth (perhaps from labrys, the double axe so common in Minoan religious art) and an Athens paying tribute to Crete may preserve distant memories of Minoan power.

Mycenaean Greece (c. 1600–1100 BCE)

On the mainland, a warrior aristocracy emerged around 1600 BCE. Its wealth burst into view when Heinrich Schliemann opened the shaft graves of Grave Circle A at Mycenae in 1876. They held gold masks, inlaid daggers and amber from the Baltic. Schliemann reportedly announced that he had “gazed upon the face of Agamemnon.” In fact the “Mask of Agamemnon” dates to around 1550 BCE, centuries before any plausible Trojan War. Over the following centuries the Mycenaeans built palaces at Mycenae, Tiryns, Pylos (the “Palace of Nestor”), Thebes and elsewhere. They also built enormous beehive tholos tombs like the so-called Treasury of Atreus, and walls of such huge stones that later Greeks thought only the one-eyed Cyclopes could have lifted them. Mycenae’s Lion Gate (c. 1250 BCE) is the oldest monumental sculpture in Europe still standing in place.

The great breakthrough came in 1952, when the architect Michael Ventris, soon working with the philologist John Chadwick, deciphered Linear B, the script on clay tablets from Knossos and Pylos, as an early form of Greek. The tablets are bureaucratic inventories: sheep, chariot wheels, perfumed oil, bronze allocations, slaves and offerings to the gods. Those gods include familiar names: Zeus, Hera, Poseidon, Hermes and even Dionysus (di-wo-nu-so). They reveal a highly centralized palace economy under a king (wanax) with a war-leader (lawagetas). Ironically, the tablets survived because the fires that destroyed the palaces baked them hard.

Troy: legend and archaeology

In Greek legend Paris, prince of Troy, carried off Helen, wife of Menelaus of Sparta. Menelaus’s brother Agamemnon, king of Mycenae, led a thousand ships to besiege Troy for ten years until the city fell to the trick of the wooden horse. Ancient Greeks dated the fall to around 1184 BCE (Eratosthenes’ reckoning). Was there a real war? The site of Hisarlik in northwest Turkey, identified by Frank Calvert and dug by Schliemann from 1870, holds a stack of cities numbered Troy I–IX. Troy VI and VIIa (c. 1300–1180 BCE) were substantial fortified cities with a large lower town, and Troy VIIa was destroyed by fire. Hittite archives mention a land of Ahhiyawa, almost certainly Mycenaean Greeks (“Achaeans”), active in western Anatolia, and a city called Wilusa, very plausibly Ilios. A historical core of conflict between Mycenaean Greeks and a western Anatolian kingdom is therefore entirely plausible. The Iliad, however, was composed some five centuries later and mixes Bronze Age memories with Iron Age realities, so it is not a war report.

The Bronze Age Collapse (c. 1200–1100 BCE)

Within a few generations around 1200 BCE, the interconnected world of the eastern Mediterranean fell apart. Pylos burned around 1180 BCE. Mycenae, Tiryns and Thebes suffered destructions. Across the Aegean the Hittite empire vanished and the great port of Ugarit was destroyed. Egypt’s Ramesses III boasted of repelling the “Sea Peoples” (around 1177 BCE in one widely used chronology). The causes were probably many and mutually reinforcing: drought (supported by climate proxies), earthquakes, migrations and raiding, internal revolt, and the fragility of over-centralized palace economies that depended on long-distance trade for tin and copper. Writing disappeared from Greece, and with it the palaces, the bureaucrats and the monumental art.

Early Iron Age · c. 1100–750 BCE

The Dark Age and the Return of the Muses

Population fell and writing was lost. Iron, a new alphabet and the epics of Homer emerged from the same centuries.

The four centuries after the collapse are often called the Greek Dark Age, now more neutrally the Early Iron Age. “Dark” describes our evidence as much as the period itself. Population fell sharply, many sites were abandoned, and settlements shrank to scattered villages. Ironworking spread, probably from Cyprus, and iron gradually replaced bronze for tools and weapons. Later Greeks told of a “Dorian invasion” or “Return of the Heracleidae” that brought Doric-speaking peoples into the Peloponnese, and of an Ionian migration across the Aegean to the coast of Asia Minor (later “Ionia”). Archaeology neither neatly confirms nor wholly disproves these stories, but the dialect map of later Greece does reflect real population movements.

The darkness was not total. At Lefkandi on Euboea, archaeologists found a huge apsidal building of around 950 BCE, nearly 50 m long. Beneath it a warrior and a richly adorned woman were buried along with four horses and imported goods from the Near East. It shows that wealth and long-distance contact survived. Pottery evolved from Protogeometric to the elegant Geometric style, with its meanders, zigzags and, by the 8th century, funeral scenes on giant grave markers like the Dipylon amphora (c. 760–750 BCE).

The alphabet

Sometime around 800–750 BCE, Greeks in contact with Phoenician traders adapted the Phoenician script to write Greek. Phoenician had letters only for consonants. The Greeks repurposed some consonant signs for sounds Greek didn’t need, such as aleph becoming alpha, which gave them the first true alphabet with full vowels. The letter names betray their Semitic origins: alpha, beta, gamma, delta from aleph, beth, gimel, daleth. Among the earliest Greek inscriptions are the Dipylon oinochoe from Athens (c. 740 BCE), which promises itself as a prize to the dancer who “dances most gracefully,” and “Nestor’s Cup” from Pithekoussai off Naples (c. 740–720 BCE), which jokes in verse about Nestor’s legendary cup. The alphabet spread via Greek colonies to the Etruscans and Romans, and from them to most of the world. A later eastern form also became the basis of Cyrillic.

Homer and Hesiod

The Iliad and the Odyssey are the summit of a long oral tradition of sung poetry in dactylic hexameter. Bards (aoidoi) composed in performance using formulas like “rosy-fingered Dawn” and “swift-footed Achilles.” Most scholars date the poems roughly to 750–650 BCE. Whether “Homer” was one genius, two, or the name for a tradition is the old “Homeric question.” Milman Parry and Albert Lord showed in the 1930s how the poems worked as oral composition by comparing them with living South Slavic epic singers. The Iliad covers just a few weeks in the tenth year of the Trojan War, centred on Achilles’ wrath. The Odyssey follows Odysseus’ ten-year voyage home to Ithaca and his faithful, cunning wife Penelope. For later Greeks these poems were scripture, encyclopedia and schoolbook together.

Hesiod, a Boeotian farmer-poet of around 700 BCE, gives us the Theogony, a genealogy of the gods from Chaos to Zeus’ victory over the Titans, and Works and Days, a gruff manual of farming and morality. It contains the myth of Pandora and the five Ages of Man. Hesiod’s complaints about “gift-devouring lords” who twist justice hint at the social tensions that would shape the Archaic polis.

Archaic Period · c. 750–480 BCE

The Age of Experiment

In three centuries the Greeks invented the polis, spread across the Mediterranean, codified law, founded the Olympics and created democracy.

The rise of the polis

The polis (plural poleis) was the defining institution of Greek life. It is usually translated “city-state,” but it meant a self-governing community of citizens: an urban centre with its acropolis, agora and temples, plus the surrounding farmland (the chora) and villages. Most poleis were small. Many had only a few hundred to a few thousand citizens, and only a handful, such as Athens, Sparta, Syracuse, Corinth and Argos, grew truly large. What mattered was that the polis belonged to its citizens. Aristotle called man “by nature a political animal” (πολιτικὸν ζῷον), meaning a creature made to live in a polis. Our words politics, policy and police all descend from it.

Colonization: frogs around a pond

From about 750 to 550 BCE, Greek cities sent out hundreds of apoikiai (“homes away”). These colonies were independent new poleis, usually led by a founder (oikistes), often with the blessing of the Delphic oracle, and bound to the mother city (metropolis) by sentiment and cult rather than rule. The motives were land hunger, trade, political strife and adventure. The Euboeans founded Pithekoussai (c. 770) and Cumae on the Bay of Naples. Corinth founded Syracuse (733), which became the greatest city of the Greek West. Southern Italy filled with so many rich cities, including Taras (Sparta’s only colony, 706), Sybaris, Croton, Rhegion and Elea, that it was called Megale Hellas, “Great Greece” (Latin Magna Graecia). Phocaeans founded Massalia (Marseille, c. 600). Therans founded Cyrene in Libya (c. 631). Megara founded Byzantium (traditionally 667), and Miletus ringed the Black Sea with dozens of trading cities such as Sinope, Olbia and Panticapaeum. Greeks also traded at Naucratis in Egypt and Al Mina in Syria.

Hoplites and tyrants

During the 7th century a new way of fighting spread. Citizen hoplites, heavily armed infantry named from their equipment (hopla), fought shoulder to shoulder in a dense phalanx, each man’s round shield half-covering his neighbour. (The Chigi vase, c. 650 BCE, is an early depiction.) Because farmers who could afford armour now formed the backbone of the army, some historians argue that they demanded a greater political voice. In many cities ambitious aristocrats exploited popular discontent to seize power as tyrants (tyrannos, probably a Lydian loanword). At first the word meant a ruler who had taken power outside traditional rules, not necessarily a cruel one. Cypselus and his son Periander ruled Corinth (c. 657–583). Polycrates made Samos a naval power, and Peisistratus ruled Athens. Many tyrants built temples, aqueducts and festivals, but by around 500 BCE the word was becoming a byword for oppression.

Gods, games and the oracle

The Greeks met as Greeks at the great Panhellenic sanctuaries. The Olympic Games, held for Zeus at Olympia every four years, were traditionally founded in 776 BCE, when Coroebus of Elis won the stadion footrace (about 192 m). Over time wrestling, boxing, the pentathlon, chariot racing and the brutal pankration were added. A sacred truce let athletes travel safely, and victors won only an olive wreath, along with lifelong glory and, often, free meals from their city. Greeks later counted time in four-year Olympiads. The Pythian (Delphi), Isthmian (Corinth) and Nemean games completed the “circuit” (periodos).

At Delphi, on the slopes of Parnassus at what Greeks called the navel (omphalos) of the world, the Pythia, Apollo’s priestess, delivered oracles that kings and cities sought on colonies, wars and laws. Famous responses were often ambiguous. Croesus of Lydia was told that if he attacked Persia, “a great empire would fall.” It did: his own. Above the temple were carved maxims including ΓΝΩΘΙ ΣΕΑΥΤΟΝ (“Know thyself”) and ΜΗΔΕΝ ΑΓΑΝ (“Nothing in excess”).

Sparta: the soldier-state

Sparta took its own path. After conquering neighbouring Messenia in the First and Second Messenian Wars (c. 735–715 and c. 660–650 BCE), the Spartans reduced its people to helots, state-owned serfs who worked the land and greatly outnumbered their masters. To hold them down, Sparta turned itself into a permanent military camp. The reforms were attributed to the semi-legendary lawgiver Lycurgus and enshrined in an oracle-sanctioned “Great Rhetra.” Sparta had two hereditary kings (of the Agiad and Eurypontid houses) who led in war; a council of elders (gerousia: 28 men over 60, plus the kings); an assembly of citizens (apella); and five annually elected ephors with sweeping powers. Male citizens (Spartiates) were forbidden to farm or trade. They ate at common messes (syssitia) and trained for war from childhood. The perioikoi (“dwellers around”) were free but non-citizen inhabitants of Laconia who handled trade and crafts.

Athens: from Draco to democracy

Athens moved in the opposite direction, though slowly and with violence along the way. In 621/0 BCE Draco gave Athens its first written laws. They were so harsh (“written in blood,” a later orator quipped) that we still say “draconian,” but writing laws down at all limited aristocratic arbitrariness. His homicide law, which distinguished intentional from unintentional killing, endured for centuries.

By the early 6th century Athens was near civil war as poor farmers fell into debt and even slavery. In 594/3 BCE the poet-statesman Solon was given power to mediate. His seisachtheia (“shaking-off of burdens”) cancelled debts secured on land and persons and abolished debt-slavery for Athenians. He divided citizens into four property classes (the pentakosiomedimnoi, hippeis, zeugitai and thetes) and tied political office to wealth rather than birth. He gave every citizen the right to appeal to a popular court and to prosecute wrongs on behalf of others. He then left Athens for ten years so that his laws would not be pressured into change.

Strife returned. The aristocrat Peisistratus made himself tyrant, finally securing power in 546 and ruling until 527. He was relatively benign: he promoted the Panathenaic festival, public works and the cult of Dionysus. His sons Hippias and Hipparchus succeeded him. Hipparchus was murdered in 514 by Harmodius and Aristogeiton, later honoured as “tyrant-slayers,” and Hippias was expelled in 510 with Spartan help.

Then, in 508/7 BCE, Cleisthenes rallied the ordinary people (demos) and enacted the reforms that founded democracy (demokratia, “people-power”). He replaced the old kinship tribes with ten new tribes, each mixing citizens from the city, the coast and the inland. Their building blocks were some 139 local villages or wards called demes, where citizens were registered. A Council of 500 (fifty from each tribe, chosen by lot) prepared business for the sovereign Assembly (ekklesia) of all adult male citizens. Ostracism, a vote with potsherds (ostraka) to exile a too-powerful man for ten years, is usually attributed to him and was first used in 487. Athens had become the world’s first known democracy. It was radical, participatory and, by modern standards, sharply exclusive, since women, slaves and resident foreigners had no vote.

Η΄

499–479 BCE

The Persian Wars

The largest empire the world had yet seen against a quarrelsome handful of Greek cities, as told by Herodotus.

By 540 BCE the Achaemenid Persian Empire of Cyrus the Great had swallowed Lydia and with it the Greek cities of Ionia. Under Darius I (r. 522–486) it stretched from the Indus to the Aegean. Persia ruled tolerantly by ancient standards but installed pro-Persian tyrants in the Greek cities.

The Ionian Revolt (499–494 BCE)

In 499 Aristagoras of Miletus led the Ionian cities in revolt. Sparta refused to help. Athens sent twenty ships and Eretria five, and in 498 the rebels burned the Lydian capital Sardis. Persia struck back methodically. The Ionian fleet was crushed at Lade (494) and Miletus was sacked, its people enslaved or deported. When the Athenian playwright Phrynichus staged The Capture of Miletus, the audience wept and he was fined for reminding them of “their own misfortunes.” Darius did not forget Athens. Herodotus says he had a servant remind him three times at dinner: “Master, remember the Athenians.”

Marathon (490 BCE)

After an expedition under Mardonius was wrecked off Mount Athos in 492, a Persian force under Datis and Artaphernes sailed across the Aegean in 490, sacked Eretria and landed at the bay of Marathon, 40 km from Athens. Some 10,000 Athenian hoplites and about 1,000 Plataeans faced a larger Persian army. The Spartans were delayed by a religious festival, the Carneia. Urged on by the general Miltiades, the Athenians thinned their centre, strengthened their wings and charged at the run. The wings closed in and routed the Persians. Herodotus gives the Persian dead as 6,400 against 192 Athenians, who were buried under the mound (soros) that still stands on the plain. The runner Pheidippides really did run to Sparta, about 240 km, to ask for help. The romantic tale of a messenger who ran from Marathon to Athens, cried “We have won!” and died is a later legend (it appears in Plutarch and Lucian), but it inspired the modern marathon race in 1896.

Thermopylae and Artemisium (480 BCE)

Darius died before he could take revenge. His son Xerxes prepared a vast invasion by land and sea. He bridged the Hellespont with boats and cut a canal through the Athos peninsula. Herodotus’ figure of over two million men is impossible, and modern estimates range from perhaps 100,000 to 300,000 soldiers. Only about thirty Greek states stood together as the “Hellenic League” under Spartan leadership. In August or September 480, King Leonidas of Sparta held the narrow coastal pass of Thermopylae (“Hot Gates”) with about 7,000 Greeks, including his bodyguard of 300 Spartiates. For two days they threw back wave after wave, including the Persian elite “Immortals.” Then a local man, Ephialtes, showed the Persians a mountain path around the pass. Leonidas sent most of the army away and stayed with his Spartans, 700 Thespians and 400 Thebans, and died fighting. Supposedly told to surrender his weapons, he answered μολὼν λαβέ, “come and take them” (the line is reported much later, by Plutarch). At sea, the Greek fleet fought the Persians to a standstill off Artemisium before withdrawing.

Salamis (480 BCE)

Athens was evacuated, and the Persians burned the Acropolis. Everything now depended on the fleet, and the fleet depended on Themistocles. In 483/2 he had persuaded the Athenians to spend a rich new strike of silver at the Laurion mines on some 200 triremes rather than dividing it among citizens. He also interpreted a Delphic oracle promising that “the wooden wall” would save Athens to mean the ships. In September 480 he lured the Persian navy into the narrow strait between the island of Salamis and the Attic coast, reportedly by sending a slave, Sicinnus, to tell Xerxes that the Greeks were about to flee. The Persian ships rowed into the narrows at dawn, lost formation and were rammed by about 370 Greek triremes. Xerxes watched from a throne on Mount Aigaleos as his fleet was shattered. Herodotus singles out Queen Artemisia of Halicarnassus, a Greek fighting for Persia, prompting Xerxes’ (possibly apocryphal) remark: “My men have become women, and my women men.”

Plataea and Mycale (479 BCE)

Xerxes withdrew to Asia but left his general Mardonius with a strong army in Greece. In 479 the largest Greek army ever assembled, about 38,000 hoplites under the Spartan regent Pausanias, met him near Plataea in Boeotia. After days of manoeuvring, the Spartan and Tegean hoplites broke the Persian line, and Mardonius was killed. Tradition says that on the same day a Greek fleet destroyed the remnants of the Persian navy at Mycale on the Ionian coast. (Another tradition has the Sicilian Greeks under Gelon of Syracuse defeating Carthage at Himera on the very day of Salamis.) The invasion was over. Aeschylus, who fought at Marathon and perhaps at Salamis, staged The Persians in 472, the oldest surviving play in the world, and Herodotus made the war the subject of the first great work of history.

Θ΄

Classical Period · 480–323 BCE

Golden Age and Civil War

Athens built the Parthenon, staged the great tragedies and debated philosophy in the agora, then spent a generation destroying itself in war with Sparta.

The Delian League becomes an empire

In 478/7 BCE Athens and the Aegean cities formed an alliance to continue the war against Persia, headquartered on the sacred island of Delos. Members contributed ships or money (the phoros), assessed by the incorruptible Aristides “the Just.” Over time most allies chose to pay rather than serve, Athens supplied the ships, and the league turned into an Athenian empire. Naxos (c. 470) and Thasos (465) tried to leave and were forced back. In 454 the treasury was moved to Athens. Athenian sea power, built on the oars of its poorest citizens, the thetes, went hand in hand with radical democracy at home. In 462/1 Ephialtes stripped the aristocratic Areopagus council of most of its powers, and pay was introduced for jurors and, later, other public service, so that poor men could afford to take part.

The age of Pericles

Pericles (c. 495–429), an aristocrat of the Alcmaeonid family and a spellbinding orator, was re-elected general (strategos) year after year from the 440s. Thucydides wrote that Athens was “in name a democracy, but in fact rule by its first citizen.” In 451/0 his citizenship law restricted citizenship to those with two Athenian parents. After a peace with Persia (the “Peace of Callias,” c. 449, whose reality is debated) he used league funds to rebuild the Acropolis.

The Parthenon (447–432 BCE), designed by Ictinus and Callicrates with sculpture supervised by Phidias, is the climax of the Doric order. It is a temple of Pentelic marble in which almost no line is truly straight. The floor curves upward at the centre, the columns swell slightly (entasis) and lean inward, and the corner columns are thicker. These refinements give the building its living tension. Its frieze showed a procession, probably the Panathenaic one, and its pediments showed the birth of Athena and her contest with Poseidon. Inside stood Phidias’ 12-metre gold-and-ivory Athena Parthenos, dedicated in 438. The Propylaea gateway (437–432), the little Temple of Athena Nike (c. 420s) and the Erechtheion with its Caryatid porch (421–406) completed the ensemble. Athens in these decades was home to Sophocles, Euripides, Socrates, Herodotus, Phidias, Hippocrates’ visitors and the sophists. No small city has ever concentrated so much talent.

“Its administration favours the many instead of the few; this is why it is called a democracy… We cultivate refinement without extravagance and knowledge without effeminacy.”

Pericles’ Funeral Oration, Thucydides 2.37–40 (trans. Crawley)

The Peloponnesian War (431–404 BCE)

“The growth of the power of Athens, and the alarm which this inspired in Sparta, made war inevitable.” So wrote Thucydides, the Athenian general-turned-historian whose account is the main source for the war, and the origin of the modern idea of the “Thucydides Trap.” Sparta led the land-based Peloponnesian League, and Athens led its naval empire. Pericles’ strategy was to abandon the countryside to Spartan invaders, shelter the population behind the Long Walls joining Athens to Piraeus, and use the fleet to raid and to keep grain flowing.

The strategy had a fatal flaw. In 430 BCE a plague broke out in the overcrowded city. It killed perhaps a quarter to a third of the population, including Pericles himself in 429. Thucydides, who caught it and survived, describes the fever, the breakdown of law and religion, and bodies piled in temples. Its identity is still debated: typhus, smallpox, measles and viral haemorrhagic fevers have all been proposed, and a 2006 DNA study of a mass grave pointed to typhoid fever, though that result has been contested.

The war ground on. Athens won a stunning success at Pylos and Sphacteria (425), where about 120 Spartiates surrendered, an unheard-of event. The Spartan general Brasidas captured Amphipolis (where Thucydides was the Athenian commander, which earned him exile). Both Brasidas and the Athenian demagogue Cleon died there in 422, and the Peace of Nicias (421) followed, a peace that did not hold. In 416 Athens besieged neutral Melos and, when it fell, killed its men and enslaved its women and children. Thucydides imagines the Athenian envoys’ chilling logic in the “Melian Dialogue”: “the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must.”

In 415 BCE, swayed by the brilliant, reckless Alcibiades, the Assembly launched the Sicilian Expedition to conquer Syracuse. Just before the fleet sailed, the sacred herms of Athens were mutilated in a single night. Alcibiades was recalled to face charges and defected to Sparta. The expedition, under the cautious Nicias, ended in catastrophe in 413. The fleet was destroyed in Syracuse harbour, the retreating army was slaughtered at the Assinarus river, and thousands of survivors were left to die in the stone quarries. Thucydides calls it the greatest disaster in Greek history. Sparta, now advised by Alcibiades, fortified Decelea in Attica, and some 20,000 slaves escaped from Athenian territory. Persian gold, channelled through the prince Cyrus the Younger, built Sparta a fleet. Athens survived an oligarchic coup (the Four Hundred, 411) and won at Arginusae (406), only to execute six of its victorious generals for failing to rescue drowning sailors. In 405 BCE the Spartan admiral Lysander caught the Athenian fleet on the beach at Aegospotami. Starved by blockade, Athens surrendered in 404. Its walls were torn down to the music of flute-girls, and a Spartan-backed junta, the Thirty Tyrants, ruled by terror until democrats under Thrasybulus restored democracy in 403.

The trial of Socrates (399 BCE)

The restored democracy is remembered above all for one verdict. In 399 the philosopher Socrates, then about seventy, was prosecuted for asebeia (impiety). The charges were “not recognizing the gods the city recognizes, introducing new divinities, and corrupting the young.” Behind them lay politics. Socrates’ former associates included Alcibiades and Critias, leader of the Thirty. A jury of 500 or 501 citizens convicted him, by 280 to 220 according to Plato’s Apology. Asked to propose a penalty, Socrates suggested free meals in the town hall for life, and the jury chose death. He refused his friends’ plan of escape and drank hemlock in prison, as Plato movingly describes in the Phaedo. His death made Plato a philosopher and haunted Athenian democracy’s reputation ever after.

Spartan and Theban hegemonies (404–362 BCE)

Victory made Sparta master of Greece, but Sparta proved a heavy-handed and shrinking hegemon. The number of full Spartiate citizens fell from about 8,000 in 480 to perhaps 1,500 by 371. The Corinthian War (395–387) ended with the King’s Peace of 387/6, dictated by the Persian king, who regained the Ionian cities. In 371 BCE at Leuctra, the Theban general Epaminondas massed his left wing fifty ranks deep, spearheaded by the elite Sacred Band of 150 paired lovers, and smashed the Spartan right. The myth of Spartan invincibility died on that field. Epaminondas invaded Laconia, freed the Messenian helots and founded the city of Messene (369), crippling Sparta’s economy for good. Theban dominance ended when Epaminondas fell in victory at Mantinea in 362. Xenophon’s history ends with the verdict that after the battle there was “even more confusion and disorder in Greece than before.” Into that vacuum stepped Macedon.

Ι΄

359–301 BCE

Macedon and Alexander

A semi-Greek northern kingdom that the southern Greeks looked down on conquered Greece, then Persia, then the known world as far as India.

Philip II (r. 359–336 BCE)

Macedon was a land of horses, timber and feuding barons, ruled by kings who claimed descent from Heracles and were admitted to the Olympic Games as Greeks, though many southern Greeks considered them half-barbarian. Philip II had spent his youth as a hostage in Thebes, watching Epaminondas. He came to power amid crisis in 359 and transformed the kingdom. He created a professional army: an infantry phalanx armed with the sarissa, a pike some 5–6 metres long, combined with the heavy Companion cavalry, light troops and siege engineers. Through gold from the Pangaeum mines, bribery, diplomacy, seven marriages and war, he expanded relentlessly. In Athens Demosthenes warned against him in fiery speeches, the Philippics, which gave English the word for an impassioned denunciation. In 338 BCE Philip crushed Athens and Thebes at Chaeronea, where his 18-year-old son Alexander led the cavalry charge that annihilated the Theban Sacred Band. Philip then organized the Greek states into the League of Corinth under his leadership and announced a war of revenge against Persia. In 336, at his daughter’s wedding in the old capital Aegae (Vergina), he was stabbed to death by a bodyguard, Pausanias. His likely tomb was discovered at Vergina in 1977.

Alexander III, “the Great” (r. 336–323 BCE)

Alexander was born in 356 BCE to Philip and the formidable Olympias of Epirus. From age 13 to 16 he was tutored by Aristotle at Mieza, and he reportedly slept with a copy of the Iliad annotated by his teacher. When Thebes revolted at the news of Philip’s death, Alexander razed it (335), sparing only the temples and the house of the poet Pindar. In spring 334 he crossed the Hellespont with about 37,000 men, visited Troy to honour Achilles, and began the most famous campaign in history. Follow it on the animated map below.

The major battles came in quick succession. At the Granicus (334) Alexander nearly died leading a cavalry charge across a river. At Issus (333) he routed the Persian king Darius III himself and captured the royal family. Before that, at Gordium, legend says he “untied” the Gordian Knot with a sword stroke. He took Tyre after a seven-month siege (332), during which he built a causeway to the island city, and then Gaza. Egypt welcomed him as a liberator and pharaoh. There he founded Alexandria (331) and trekked to the oracle of Zeus-Ammon at Siwa, which reportedly hailed him as son of the god. On 1 October 331 BCE, at Gaugamela in northern Mesopotamia, he broke Darius’ grand army. Babylon, Susa and Persepolis, with their colossal treasuries, fell in turn. Persepolis’ palace burned in 330, whether in drunken revelry (as one tradition tells) or as calculated revenge for the burning of Athens.

When Darius was murdered by his own satrap Bessus in 330, Alexander made himself the Persian king’s avenger and successor. He pressed east through modern Iran, Afghanistan, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan and Tajikistan, founding “Alexandrias” as he went (Herat, Kandahar and Alexandria Eschate, “the Furthest,” on the Jaxartes). This was a brutal guerrilla war. He married the Bactrian noblewoman Roxana (327), adopted Persian court dress and the bowing ritual of proskynesis, which his Macedonians hated, and killed his friend Cleitus in a drunken rage at Maracanda (Samarkand). In 326 he crossed into the Punjab and defeated the Indian king Porus and his war elephants at the Hydaspes (Jhelum). His beloved horse Bucephalus died there. At the Hyphasis (Beas) river his exhausted army refused to go farther, and Alexander turned back. He sailed down the Indus, was badly wounded storming a town of the Malli, and then led part of his army through the waterless Gedrosian desert (Makran), where thousands died, while his admiral Nearchus explored the coast by sea.

Back in Persia in 324, Alexander held a mass wedding at Susa, marrying two Persian princesses himself while some 80–90 of his officers took Persian brides, and he enrolled Iranians in his army. His closest companion Hephaestion died at Ecbatana that autumn, and Alexander’s grief was extravagant. On 10 or 11 June 323 BCE, in Nebuchadnezzar’s palace at Babylon, Alexander died after a short fever, a month short of his 33rd birthday. Theories range from malaria or typhoid to poison. Asked to whom he left his empire, he supposedly said: “To the strongest.”

The Diadochi: wars of the successors (323–281 BCE)

There was no adult heir. Roxana’s son, Alexander IV, was born after his father’s death, and Alexander’s half-brother Philip III Arrhidaeus was mentally impaired. The generals, the Diadochi (“successors”), carved up the empire, first as regents and then as kings in their own right. Both royal heirs were murdered by 309. Forty years of war followed among Perdiccas, Antipater, Ptolemy, Antigonus the One-Eyed and his dashing son Demetrius “the Besieger,” Seleucus, Lysimachus, Cassander and the loyal Greek secretary Eumenes. At Ipsus (301) Antigonus was killed fighting a coalition, and at Corupedium (281) Seleucus defeated and killed Lysimachus, only to be assassinated months later. What emerged were three great dynasties: the Ptolemies in Egypt, the Seleucids in Asia and the Antigonids in Macedon. Alongside them stood lesser powers such as the Attalids of Pergamon, Rhodes, the Greek kingdom of Bactria and the federal Achaean and Aetolian Leagues.

ΙΑ΄

Hellenistic Period · 323–31 BCE

A Greek World Without Borders

Greek kings ruled Egyptians, Babylonians and Bactrians. Greek became the common language from Sicily to the Indus, and Alexandria became the intellectual capital of the world.

The historian J. G. Droysen coined “Hellenistic” (Greek-ish) in the 1830s for the era between Alexander and Rome. Once dismissed as a decadent coda to the Classical age, it is now seen as one of antiquity’s most dynamic periods. Greek settlers, soldiers and administrators spread through the old Persian empire and founded or refounded cities with gymnasia and theatres. A simplified common Greek, the Koine (“common” dialect), became the lingua franca of the eastern Mediterranean. It is the language of the Septuagint translation of the Hebrew Bible, and later of the New Testament.

305–30 BCE

Ptolemaic Egypt

Founded by Ptolemy I Soter, Alexander’s general and historian. The Ptolemies ruled as pharaohs to Egyptians and Macedonian kings to Greeks, often marrying their sisters in Egyptian royal style. Their capital, Alexandria, with its lighthouse and Library, was the greatest city of the age. The dynasty ended with Cleopatra VII, reportedly the first Ptolemy to learn Egyptian.

312–63 BCE

The Seleucid Empire

The largest successor state, stretching at first from the Aegean to the Indus, governed from Antioch and Seleucia-on-the-Tigris. It gradually lost its eastern lands to Parthia and Bactria. Antiochus III “the Great” was humbled by Rome at Magnesia (190 BCE), and Antiochus IV’s persecution provoked the Maccabean revolt in Judaea (167–160 BCE). Pompey dissolved the remnant in 63 BCE.

c. 276–168 BCE

Antigonid Macedon

Antigonus II Gonatas secured Macedon after a Celtic invasion (the Gauls even attacked Delphi in 279 BCE). The Antigonids dominated mainland Greece through garrisons such as the “fetters of Greece” at Corinth, Chalcis and Demetrias, until Rome destroyed the kingdom at Pydna in 168 BCE.

c. 250 BCE–10 CE

Bactria and the Indo-Greeks

On the far frontier, in Afghanistan and Pakistan, Greek kings minted superb coins and mingled Greek and Buddhist culture. King Menander I (Milinda, c. 165–130 BCE) appears in a Buddhist dialogue, the Milindapañha. Greco-Buddhist art of Gandhara later gave the Buddha some of his earliest human images.

Alexandria and its Library

Founded by Alexander in 331, Alexandria grew into a metropolis of perhaps 300,000–500,000 people: Greeks, Egyptians and the largest Jewish community outside Judaea. The Pharos lighthouse (c. 280 BCE), perhaps 100 m tall, was one of the Seven Wonders. The first Ptolemies, perhaps advised by Demetrius of Phalerum, founded the Mouseion (“shrine of the Muses,” our “museum”), a royal research institute with salaried scholars, and the great Library. Its holdings were said to reach several hundred thousand papyrus rolls. Ancient figures vary wildly. Ships docking at Alexandria supposedly had their books copied, and the originals were kept. Scholars such as Zenodotus, Aristophanes of Byzantium and Aristarchus of Samothrace edited Homer and invented the critical apparatus of scholarship, including accent marks. The poet Callimachus compiled the Pinakes, the first great library catalogue. The Library’s end was gradual, not a single fire. Part of it may have burned when Julius Caesar was besieged in 48 BCE. It suffered under later wars and neglect, and the daughter library in the Serapeum was destroyed in 391 CE. The story that Arab conquerors burned it in 642 is a later legend.

A scientific revolution

Royal patronage made the 3rd century BCE a golden age of science, with achievements not matched in Europe until the early modern period.

fl. c. 300 BCE · Alexandria

Euclid

His Elements built geometry and number theory from definitions, postulates and rigorous proof, including the infinitude of primes. It became the most widely used textbook in history and was still taught in schools in the 20th century.

c. 287–212 BCE · Syracuse

Archimedes

He bounded π between 3 10⁄71 and 3 1⁄7, found the area and volume of the sphere, and founded statics and hydrostatics (the principle of buoyancy). He also designed war machines against Rome. He was killed when Syracuse fell in 212. “Give me a place to stand, and I will move the earth.”

c. 276–194 BCE · Alexandria

Eratosthenes

Librarian of Alexandria, he measured Earth’s circumference from the angle of the noon sun at Syene and Alexandria, obtaining 252,000 stadia. Depending on the length of stadion used, that was within a few percent of the true value. He also coined “geography” and devised the Sieve for primes.

c. 310–230 BCE · Samos

Aristarchus

He proposed a heliocentric cosmos, with Earth orbiting a central Sun, eighteen centuries before Copernicus, who knew of him. He also tried to measure the relative sizes and distances of the Sun and Moon.

c. 190–120 BCE · Rhodes

Hipparchus

He discovered the precession of the equinoxes, compiled a star catalogue with magnitudes, and developed trigonometry through a table of chords.

3rd c. BCE · Alexandria

Herophilus & Erasistratus

Pioneers of human anatomy through dissection. They distinguished nerves from tendons, sensory from motor nerves, and studied the brain, eye and heart valves.

The Antikythera mechanism

In 1900–01 sponge divers off the island of Antikythera recovered treasures from a Roman-era shipwreck (sunk c. 70–60 BCE). Among them was a corroded lump of bronze that turned out to be the world’s oldest known analogue computer. It had at least 30 surviving interlocking bronze gears in a box the size of a shoebox. Turning a handle displayed the positions of the Sun and Moon, the phase of the Moon, the 19-year Metonic cycle and the 223-month Saros eclipse cycle, and even the four-year cycle of the Panhellenic games. Reconstructions suggest it also tracked the five known planets. Its construction is usually dated to the 2nd or early 1st century BCE, and nothing of comparable mechanical complexity survives until medieval astronomical clocks.

Hellenistic art turned toward emotion, movement and realism, as in the Pergamon Altar, the Winged Victory of Samothrace and the Laocoön (see The Arts). Philosophy turned toward personal tranquillity, through the Stoics and Epicureans (see Philosophy). Cities like Rhodes, Pergamon and Antioch flourished. But the kingdoms exhausted themselves in endless wars, and a new power was rising in the west.

ΙΒ΄

229 BCE – 330 CE

Captive Greece Took Her Captor Captive

Rome conquered the Greek world in little more than a century, and Greek culture went on to shape Rome itself.

Rome entered the Greek world gradually, through wars against Illyrian pirates (229 BCE) and then against Philip V of Macedon, who had allied with Hannibal. In the Second Macedonian War the consul Flamininus defeated Philip at Cynoscephalae (197 BCE). The legions’ flexible maniples outmanoeuvred the rigid phalanx on broken ground. At the Isthmian Games of 196 Flamininus proclaimed the “freedom of the Greeks” to delirious crowds. That freedom proved conditional. Rome crushed Antiochus III at Magnesia (190). In the Third Macedonian War Aemilius Paullus destroyed the last Antigonid king, Perseus, at Pydna (168), and Macedon was split into four client republics. After a revolt, Macedonia became a Roman province in 148/7.

In 146 BCE, the same year Rome razed Carthage, the consul Lucius Mummius crushed the Achaean League and sacked Corinth. The men were killed, the women and children enslaved, and shiploads of art carried to Rome. Greece came under the governor of Macedonia and later became the province of Achaea (27 BCE). Athens backed Mithridates VI of Pontus against Rome and was brutally sacked by Sulla in 86 BCE. The Roman civil wars were fought largely on Greek soil: Pharsalus (48), Philippi (42) and finally the naval battle of Actium off western Greece on 2 September 31 BCE. There Octavian defeated Mark Antony and Cleopatra VII, last of the Ptolemies. Her suicide in 30 BCE ended the last of the Hellenistic kingdoms, and that is the conventional end of the Hellenistic age.

Graecia capta ferum victorem cepit et artes intulit agresti Latio.

“Captive Greece took her savage conqueror captive, and brought the arts to rustic Latium.”

Horace, Epistles 2.1.156

Greek culture under Rome

Rome absorbed Greek culture so thoroughly that the eastern half of the empire remained Greek-speaking throughout. Educated Romans learned Greek and studied in Athens and Rhodes. They copied Greek statues (most “Greek” sculptures we know survive as Roman marble copies of lost bronzes) and modelled their literature on Greek genres: Virgil’s Aeneid answers Homer. Greek slaves and freedmen worked as Rome’s doctors, tutors and secretaries. The Greek historian Polybius, a hostage in Rome after Pydna, wrote to explain to Greeks how Rome had conquered the world in under 53 years. Under the empire Greek culture enjoyed a renaissance, the “Second Sophistic.” Its figures include Plutarch (c. 46–after 119 CE), whose Parallel Lives paired famous Greeks and Romans and later inspired Shakespeare; the travel writer Pausanias, whose Description of Greece is still used by archaeologists; the physician Galen; the astronomer Ptolemy; and the satirist Lucian.

The philhellene emperor Hadrian (r. 117–138) lavished buildings on Athens, completed the colossal Temple of Olympian Zeus begun under Peisistratus six centuries earlier, and founded a league of Greek cities, the Panhellenion. Saint Paul preached in Athens, reputedly from the Areopagus hill, and in Corinth, and the New Testament was written in Koine Greek. Invasions such as the Heruli’s sack of Athens in 267 CE marked the troubles of the 3rd century. In 330 CE Constantine dedicated a new capital on the site of the old Greek colony of Byzantium. Constantinople would carry the Greek-speaking Roman world forward for another eleven centuries (see the Epilogue).

ΙΓ΄

Society & Daily Life

Citizens, Women, Slaves and Strangers

Who belonged to the polis, who did the work, and what people ate, wore and argued about.

Every polis divided its inhabitants sharply. At the top were the citizens (politai). Full political rights belonged only to adult males, but citizen women mattered too, because they alone could bear legitimate citizen children. Below them, in commercial cities like Athens, lived the metics (metoikoi), free foreigners, often Greeks from other cities, who paid a special tax, served in the army, and could not own land or vote. The orator Lysias and the philosopher Aristotle were both metics in Athens. At the bottom were slaves. The two greatest cities illustrate how differently the Greeks could organize the same basic structure.

Athens

Sea power · democracy · the “school of Hellas”

vs

Sparta

Land power · oligarchy · the soldier-state

Direct democracy after 508/7. The Assembly of all adult male citizens met about 40 times a year, the Council of 500 was chosen by lot, and juries of hundreds sat in the courts. Most offices were filled by lottery. Generals were elected.
Government
A mixed constitution: two hereditary kings, a council of 28 elders over 60 (gerousia), five annually elected ephors and a citizen assembly that voted by shouting. Ancient writers praised its stability.
Trade, crafts, silver from Laurion, olive oil and pottery exports, imperial tribute. Coined money: the famous silver “owls.” Grain was imported from the Black Sea and Egypt.
Economy
Agrarian, worked by helots. Citizens were barred from trade, and iron bars were the traditional currency, though Sparta did use foreign coin. The perioikoi handled crafts and commerce.
The navy was the core of its power, rowed by poorer citizens (thetes), metics and hired rowers. Citizen hoplite militia. Two years of ephebic training for young men by the 4th century.
Military
Greece’s only professional citizen army: full-time hoplites in red cloaks, drilled in formation and famed for discipline. Shield emblem: the Λ (lambda) for Lakedaimon, at least by the late 5th century.
Private and fee-based: letters, music, poetry (Homer) and athletics for boys of families who could afford it. Later came sophists, rhetoric and philosophical schools such as the Academy and Lyceum.
Education
The state-run agoge, from age 7: boys lived in barracks, went hungry, were taught to steal food without being caught, endured flogging, and learned laconic speech. Basic literacy only.
Citizen women were largely confined to the household, had male guardians (kyrioi), married at around 14, and had no political rights. Their main public role was in religion, including priestesshoods and festivals such as the Thesmophoria.
Women
Unusually free by Greek standards: girls exercised publicly, women could own and inherit property (Aristotle claims they held two-fifths of the land) and managed estates while men were at war. “Come back with your shield or on it.”
Perhaps 30,000–60,000 adult male citizens in the 5th century, out of an Attic population of perhaps 200,000–350,000. From 451/0, citizenship required two Athenian parents.
Citizens
About 8,000 Spartiates in 480 BCE, falling to perhaps 1,500 by 371. Citizenship depended on completing the agoge and paying mess dues. Those who failed lost status (becoming “inferiors”).
Chattel slaves, privately owned and bought and sold, perhaps 60,000–100,000 or more. They worked in households, workshops and the brutal Laurion silver mines. Some publicly owned slaves served as clerks and police.
Unfree labour
Helots, a whole subject population of Laconia and Messenia owned collectively by the state. They far outnumbered citizens and lived under terror: the ephors ritually declared war on them each year, and the krypteia hunted suspects.
The Parthenon, tragedy and comedy, philosophy, history, rhetoric. Open to outsiders: “we throw open our city to the world” (Pericles).
Culture
Choral poetry and music flourished in the 7th century (Alcman, Tyrtaeus). Later the state became austere, suspicious of foreigners and periodically expelled them (xenelasia). Few monuments survive.

Women

Our sources are almost all written by men, and Athenian ones dominate. The Athenian ideal, voiced by Pericles, was that a woman’s greatest glory was “to be least talked about among men, whether for praise or blame.” Respectable women spent much time in the women’s quarters (gynaikonitis), spinning and weaving, managing slaves and stores, and raising children. Poorer women sold goods in the market, worked as wet-nurses or in the fields. Yet women were indispensable in religion. The priestess of Athena Polias was one of the most prestigious figures in Athens, and the Pythia spoke for Apollo. Some women broke through the silence of the record: Sappho, the greatest lyric poet of Greece; Aspasia of Miletus, Pericles’ brilliant partner; the Cynic philosopher Hipparchia; and the Hellenistic queens Arsinoë II and Cleopatra VII, who ruled empires. Hetairai, educated courtesans, enjoyed a social freedom denied to wives but lived precarious lives.

Slavery

Slavery was everywhere in the Greek world and almost never questioned. Aristotle notoriously argued that some people were “slaves by nature,” though some sophists objected that nature makes no one a slave. Slaves came from war captives, piracy, the trade with Thrace and the Black Sea, and the children of slaves. Their conditions ranged from trusted household servants and skilled craftsmen, some of whom lived independently and could buy their freedom, to the miners of Laurion, chained and worked to death in narrow galleries. Greek freedom and Greek slavery grew up together, an uncomfortable fact for anyone celebrating the Greek invention of liberty.

Education and coming of age

An Athenian boy of means was escorted to school by a slave paidagogos (source of “pedagogue”). There he learned letters from the grammatistes, the lyre and poetry from the kitharistes, and wrestling and athletics from the paidotribes at the palaestra. Memorizing Homer was central. The sophists of the 5th century sold advanced training in rhetoric for high fees. In the 4th century Isocrates’ school of rhetoric, Plato’s Academy (founded c. 387 BCE) and Aristotle’s Lyceum (335 BCE) created the first institutions of higher learning. Girls were generally educated at home in household skills, though some learned to read.

The economy

The ancient Greek economy was fundamentally agrarian. Most citizens were farmers, and land was the most respectable form of wealth. But trade was vital. Coinage, invented in neighbouring Lydia around 600 BCE from electrum, a natural gold-silver alloy, spread rapidly through Greek cities, and each polis struck its own coins with civic emblems: Athena’s owl, the turtle of Aegina, the Pegasus of Corinth. Athens’ silver “owls” from the Laurion mines became the first international currency of the Mediterranean. Piraeus was the great entrepôt, with banks, often run by former slaves such as the famous Pasion, and maritime loans. Grain imports from the Black Sea were protected by law, with harsh penalties for profiteering.

Daily life: house, food and clothing

Houses were modest, built of mudbrick on stone foundations around an inner courtyard with an altar. Rooms opened onto the court. The andron (men’s room) hosted the symposium, an evening drinking party where reclining men mixed wine with water in a krater, sang, played drinking games such as kottabos (flicking wine dregs at a target), recited poetry and debated. Plato’s Symposium is an imagined one devoted to love. Drinking unmixed wine was considered barbaric.

Food was simple and largely plant-based. Staples were barley porridge or flatcakes (maza) and wheat bread for those who could afford it, with olives and oil, figs, grapes, lentils, chickpeas and beans, onions, garlic, cheese (mostly goat and sheep), honey, and fish, both fresh and salted. Meat was eaten mainly after religious sacrifices, when the community feasted on the animal while the gods received the smoke of the thigh-bones wrapped in fat. Spartans ate the infamous “black broth” (melas zomos), pork cooked in blood and vinegar. A visitor who tasted it supposedly remarked that now he understood why Spartans did not fear death.

Clothing was based on rectangles of wool or linen, draped and pinned rather than tailored. Women wore the peplos (a heavy woollen garment folded and pinned at the shoulders, the older “Doric” style) or the lighter linen chiton, with a himation mantle over it. Men wore a shorter chiton and himation. Young men and riders wore the short chlamys cloak and the broad-brimmed petasos hat. Many people went barefoot at home and wore leather sandals outdoors. Ancient garments, like ancient statues, were brightly coloured. Purple dye from murex shells was the costliest of all.

ΙΔ΄

Religion & Mythology

Gods, Titans and Heroes

There was no church, no creed and no single holy book, only sacrifice, festival and a vast, contradictory web of stories.

Greek religion was about practice more than belief. Piety meant honouring the gods with the right rituals at the right time: prayer, libation, and above all animal sacrifice (thysia) at an outdoor altar before a temple. The temple itself was the god’s house, not a congregation hall. Priests and priestesses were usually part-time citizens, not a separate caste. The gods were immortal, powerful and very human in their passions. Myths explained the world and justified rituals, and they existed in many local versions. Hesiod and Homer, Herodotus says, “gave the gods their names, honours and forms.”

From Chaos to Olympus

In Hesiod’s Theogony, first came Chaos (the Chasm), then Gaia (Earth), Tartarus and Eros. Gaia bore Uranus (Sky), and from their union came the twelve Titans. The youngest, Cronus, castrated his father with a sickle at Gaia’s urging and took power. Warned that a child would overthrow him, Cronus swallowed each child his sister-wife Rhea bore: Hestia, Demeter, Hera, Hades and Poseidon. Rhea saved the last, Zeus, by hiding him in a cave on Crete and giving Cronus a stone wrapped in swaddling clothes. Grown, Zeus forced Cronus to disgorge his siblings, and after a ten-year war, the Titanomachy, the Olympians imprisoned the Titans in Tartarus. Zeus, Poseidon and Hades drew lots for the sky, the sea and the underworld. The Titan Prometheus, who stole fire for humankind, was chained to a rock where an eagle ate his liver daily. His brother Atlas was condemned to hold up the sky.

Heroes

Between gods and mortals stood the heroes, often sons of gods, whose deeds filled epic and tragedy and whose tombs received cult worship.

Son of Zeus & Alcmene

Heracles

The strongest of men. Driven mad by Hera, he killed his own children, and in penance performed the Twelve Labours for King Eurystheus: the Nemean lion, the Hydra, the Augean stables, Cerberus and more. At his death he was made a god on Olympus. The Romans called him Hercules.

Son of Zeus & Danaë

Perseus

With winged sandals, a cap of invisibility and Athena’s polished shield, he beheaded the Gorgon Medusa, whose gaze turned men to stone. Returning, he rescued Andromeda from a sea monster. He was the legendary founder of Mycenae.

Son of Aegeus (or Poseidon)

Theseus

Athens’ national hero. He slew the Minotaur in the Cretan labyrinth with the help of Ariadne’s thread, then abandoned her on Naxos. His father, seeing the black sail Theseus forgot to change, threw himself into the sea that now bears his name. Athenians credited Theseus with uniting Attica.

Son of Peleus & Thetis

Achilles

The greatest warrior at Troy, who chose a short glorious life over a long obscure one. His wrath at Agamemnon, the death of his companion Patroclus and his vengeance on Hector form the arc of the Iliad. He was later killed by Paris’ arrow; the story of the vulnerable heel is post-Homeric.

Son of Laertes, king of Ithaca

Odysseus

The man of “many turns,” whose wooden horse took Troy. His ten-year voyage home took him past the Cyclops Polyphemus, the witch Circe, the land of the dead, the Sirens, and Scylla and Charybdis, back to Penelope and a bloody reckoning with her suitors.

Son of Aeson, prince of Iolcus

Jason

Leader of the Argonauts on the quest for the Golden Fleece in Colchis, on the eastern Black Sea, won with the help of the sorceress Medea. His later betrayal of her is the subject of Euripides’ Medea.

Festivals

The Athenian calendar was crowded with festivals, perhaps 120 festival days a year. The Panathenaia honoured Athena’s birthday each summer, and every fourth year the Great Panathenaia added athletic and musical contests (prizes were amphorae of sacred olive oil) and a grand procession that carried a new woven peplos to the goddess’s ancient statue. The City Dionysia in spring staged the tragedies and comedies. Women alone celebrated the Thesmophoria for Demeter, a fertility festival. The Anthesteria opened new wine and welcomed the wandering spirits of the dead.

Mystery cults

For those who wanted a more personal religion, with the promise of a better afterlife, there were the mysteries, secret rites of initiation. The most famous were the Eleusinian Mysteries of Demeter and Persephone, celebrating the mother’s grief and her daughter’s return from the underworld, the myth of the seasons. Initiates walked in procession along the Sacred Way from Athens to Eleusis. Slaves and foreigners, women and men could all be initiated, and the secret was so well kept that we still do not know exactly what happened in the Telesterion hall. The rites lasted nearly two thousand years until suppressed under Theodosius I in the 390s CE. Other mystery cults included the Cabeiri of Samothrace, the frenzied rites of Dionysus and the ascetic, reincarnation-minded Orphics. In Hellenistic and Roman times imported cults joined them, such as those of Isis and Cybele.

Oracles and healing

Beyond Delphi, Greeks consulted Zeus at Dodona in Epirus, the oldest oracle, where priests interpreted the rustling of a sacred oak. Thousands of inscribed lead tablets with questions survive, such as “Is the child she carries mine?” and “Should I take up sheep-farming?” The healing god Asclepius received the sick at sanctuaries like Epidaurus, where patients slept in a sacred hall, hoping for healing dreams, and left thank-offerings in the shape of cured body parts.

ΙΕ΄

Philosophy · c. 600–200 BCE

The Love of Wisdom

The Greeks began explaining nature through reasoned argument instead of myth, and Western philosophy grew out of that habit.

The Presocratics

In the early 6th century BCE, in the rich Ionian city of Miletus, Thales (c. 624–546) asked what everything is ultimately made of and answered: water. The answer matters less than the question and the method. Thales sought a natural principle (arche) rather than a divine story, and he was said to have predicted the solar eclipse of 585 BCE. His successors argued with him and with each other. Anaximander proposed the boundless (apeiron), drew the first Greek map of the world and imagined Earth floating unsupported in space. Anaximenes chose air. Pythagoras of Samos (c. 570–495) founded a semi-religious brotherhood at Croton in southern Italy that taught the transmigration of souls and found the cosmos ordered by number and musical ratio. The “Pythagorean” theorem was known in Babylon a thousand years earlier, but proving such things became a Greek obsession.

Heraclitus of Ephesus (fl. c. 500), “the Obscure,” taught that all is flux governed by a rational principle, the logos: “you cannot step twice into the same river.” Parmenides of Elea answered that change is impossible: what is cannot come from what is not, so reality is one, unchanging and eternal, and the senses deceive. His pupil Zeno defended him with paradoxes such as Achilles and the tortoise, and the arrow that cannot move. Empedocles answered with four eternal elements (earth, water, air and fire) moved by Love and Strife. Anaxagoras, who brought philosophy to Athens, posited a cosmic Mind (nous) and was prosecuted for calling the Sun a red-hot stone. Leucippus and Democritus made the boldest move: everything consists of indivisible particles, atomoi, moving in the void.

Meanwhile the sophists, professional teachers of rhetoric like Protagoras (“Man is the measure of all things”) and Gorgias, turned attention from nature to human society. They asked whether laws and morals were natural (physis) or merely convention (nomos).

Socrates, Plato, Aristotle

Socrates (469–399) wrote nothing. We know him through his students Plato and Xenophon and through Aristophanes’ mockery in Clouds. He questioned anyone in the agora about justice, courage and piety, exposing their confusion and professing his own ignorance. The Delphic oracle, he said, had called him the wisest of men only because he knew that he did not know. His method of relentless question and answer (the elenchus) and his insistence on examining one’s life changed philosophy for ever.

ὁ δὲ ἀνεξέταστος βίος οὐ βιωτὸς ἀνθρώπῳ

“The unexamined life is not worth living for a human being.”

Socrates, in Plato’s Apology 38a

Plato (c. 428–348) wrote some thirty philosophical dialogues, most starring Socrates, and founded the Academy, which lasted in some form for centuries. His theory of Forms holds that the changing world of the senses is a shadow of eternal, perfect realities grasped by reason, as in the allegory of the cave in the Republic. In the same work he sketched an ideal state ruled by philosopher-kings. Alfred North Whitehead famously called European philosophy “a series of footnotes to Plato.”

Aristotle (384–322), from Stagira, studied at the Academy for twenty years, tutored Alexander and founded the Lyceum in Athens, whose members were called “Peripatetics” from the covered walkway (peripatos) where they taught. His range is staggering: logic (he invented the syllogism), physics, metaphysics, biology (he dissected and classified hundreds of animals), ethics (virtue as a mean between extremes, and happiness, eudaimonia, as the goal of life), politics (he collected 158 city constitutions), rhetoric and poetics. Medieval scholars simply called him “the Philosopher.”

Hellenistic schools: how to live

In the uncertain world after Alexander, philosophy turned toward the art of living well. The Cynics, led by Diogenes of Sinope, who lived in a storage jar and told Alexander to stand out of his sunlight, rejected convention entirely and lived “according to nature.” The Stoics, founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BCE and named for the Painted Stoa in Athens where he taught, held that virtue is the only good, that the universe is governed by divine reason, and that we should accept with equanimity what we cannot control. Through Romans like Seneca, Epictetus and the emperor Marcus Aurelius, Stoicism remains popular today. The Epicureans, founded by Epicurus in his Garden, adapted atomism. The gods do not intervene, death is nothing to us, and the goal of life is pleasure understood as tranquillity (ataraxia) and freedom from pain, not indulgence. The Skeptics, from Pyrrho onward, suspended judgment altogether.

ΙϚ΄

Drama · Poetry · History · Sculpture · Pottery · Architecture

The Arts of the Muses

Tragedy and comedy, lyric poetry, the writing of history, and a revolution in how the human body was carved, painted and built around.

Drama and the theatre

Western drama was born in Athens, in the worship of Dionysus. At the City Dionysia each spring, three tragedians each presented three tragedies and a bawdy satyr play over successive days, judged by citizens chosen by lot. Comedies had their own competition. Tradition says Thespis (hence “thespian”) first stepped out of the chorus as an actor around 534 BCE. All actors were men in masks, and a chorus sang and danced in the circular orchestra (“dancing place”). Behind it stood the skene (stage building, our “scene”). Spectators sat on the hillside theatron (“viewing place”). A crane (mechane) lowered gods onto the stage, which gave us deus ex machina. The 4th-century theatre at Epidaurus, seating around 13,000–14,000, has acoustics so fine that a coin dropped in the orchestra can be heard in the top rows.

c. 525–456 BCE · 7 plays survive

Aeschylus

Veteran of Marathon. He added the second actor, which made true dialogue possible. His Oresteia (458), the only complete surviving trilogy, moves from a cycle of family murder to the founding of a law court on the Areopagus. His epitaph mentions only his service at Marathon.

c. 497–406 BCE · 7 plays survive

Sophocles

Added the third actor and painted scenery. He won about 24 victories and never placed last. Oedipus the King, which Aristotle held up as the model tragedy, and Antigone, the clash of conscience with state, are his masterpieces. He wrote around 120 plays.

c. 480–406 BCE · 18 or 19 survive

Euripides

The restless questioner who put women, slaves and the suffering of war at the centre: Medea (431), Trojan Women (415, staged months after Melos), and the Bacchae. Less successful in his lifetime, he was the most performed tragedian after his death.

c. 446–386 BCE · 11 plays survive

Aristophanes

The master of Old Comedy: obscene, fantastical and fiercely political. In Clouds (423) he lampooned Socrates, in Lysistrata (411) women stage a sex strike to end the war, and in Frogs (405) Dionysus goes to Hades to bring back a good tragedian. Later, Menander’s gentler New Comedy shaped Roman and modern sitcom plots.

Poetry

Beyond epic, the Archaic age produced brilliant lyric poetry, sung to the lyre. Sappho of Lesbos (c. 630–570 BCE), whom Plato reportedly called “the tenth Muse,” wrote of desire, jealousy and memory among a circle of women. Most of her nine books are lost, and she survives in fragments, some recovered from papyrus scraps as recently as 2004 and 2014. Her contemporary Alcaeus wrote of politics and wine, and Archilochus of war and mockery. Pindar of Thebes (c. 518–438) composed soaring odes for victors at the games. Simonides wrote epitaphs for the dead of the Persian Wars. In the Hellenistic period, Theocritus invented pastoral poetry, and Apollonius of Rhodes retold the voyage of the Argonauts.

“Some say an army of horsemen, some of foot soldiers, some of ships, is the most beautiful thing on the black earth. But I say it is whatever one loves.”

Sappho, fragment 16

The invention of history

Herodotus of Halicarnassus (c. 484–425) opened his Histories by saying he wrote “so that the great and marvellous deeds of Greeks and barbarians might not lose their glory, and to show why they fought each other.” His word for “inquiry,” historia, gave us “history.” He travelled widely, collected oral traditions, weighed competing versions and digressed gloriously on Egyptian mummification and Scythian customs. Cicero called him “the father of history,” and his critics called him the father of lies. Thucydides (c. 460–400) was sterner. He sought accuracy through eyewitnesses, rejected divine explanation, analysed power with unsentimental clarity, and wrote his account of the Peloponnesian War as “a possession for all time” (κτῆμά τε ἐς αἰεὶ). Xenophon continued his story, and later Polybius and Plutarch carried the tradition into the Roman age.

Sculpture: from kouros to pathos

Greek sculpture underwent one of the great revolutions in art history in about 150 years. Archaic kouroi, nude youths, and korai, draped maidens, stood rigidly frontal, influenced by Egyptian models. They had the left foot forward, arms at the sides, and the enigmatic “Archaic smile.” Around 480 BCE the Kritios Boy shifted his weight onto one leg, and with this contrapposto the statue came alive. The Classical sculptors sought ideal proportion: Polykleitos’ Doryphoros (Spear-bearer, c. 440) embodied his treatise, the Canon. Myron’s Discobolus froze motion, and Phidias designed the Parthenon marbles. In the 4th century Praxiteles carved the Aphrodite of Knidos, the first monumental female nude, and Lysippos made slenderer figures and Alexander’s official portraits. Hellenistic sculptors pursued drama, emotion and realism: the Winged Victory of Samothrace, the agonized Laocoön, the Dying Gaul, wrinkled old market-women. And almost all of it was originally painted in vivid colours, as pigment traces confirm. The “white marble” ideal was a Renaissance and Neoclassical misconception.

Pottery: black-figure and red-figure

Greek painted pottery is our richest surviving gallery of Greek images: myths, athletes, symposia, weddings and women at the fountain. After the Geometric style (c. 900–700) and the animal friezes of the Corinthian-led Orientalizing period came two great Athenian techniques. In black-figure, perfected in Athens c. 620–480 BCE, painters drew figures as silhouettes in a fine clay slip on the orange clay, then incised details through the slip with a sharp point. Its supreme master was Exekias, whose amphora of Achilles and Ajax playing a board game (c. 540–530) is a masterpiece of suspense. Around 530 BCE Athenian potters reversed the scheme. In red-figure the figures were left in the clay colour and the background painted black, so details could be painted with a brush in flowing lines, allowing anatomy, foreshortening and emotion. Some early “bilingual” vases show the same scene in both techniques. The black was not a paint but a chemical effect of a three-stage firing.

Architecture: the orders

Greek temples followed a strict grammar of parts, the orders, later codified by the Roman architect Vitruvius. The sturdy Doric order (from about 650 BCE, on the mainland and in the West) has no base. Its fluted shaft rises straight from the stylobate to a plain cushion capital, and its frieze alternates grooved triglyphs with sculpted metopes. Examples include the Parthenon, the Temple of Hephaestus and the temples of Paestum. The slender Ionic order, from the eastern Aegean, stands on a moulded base, curls into scroll-like volutes and carries a continuous frieze. The Erechtheion and the vast Temple of Artemis at Ephesus are Ionic. The ornate Corinthian order, whose capital sprouts acanthus leaves, was supposedly inspired, Vitruvius says, by a basket left on a girl’s grave and overgrown by acanthus. It appears first at Bassae around 420 BCE and became a Roman favourite, as in the Temple of Olympian Zeus in Athens.

Science · Medicine · Mathematics

Reason Applied to Nature

Deductive proof, natural explanations of disease, and a spherical Earth measured from a shadow.

Medicine: Hippocrates and after

Hippocrates of Kos (c. 460–370 BCE) is the most famous name in ancient medicine, though the roughly sixty treatises of the Hippocratic Corpus were written by many authors over generations. Their revolutionary premise was that disease has natural causes. The treatise On the Sacred Disease insists that epilepsy is no more divine than any other illness, and that people call it sacred only because they do not understand it. Hippocratic doctors observed and recorded case histories, attended to diet, environment and regimen (Airs, Waters, Places), and gave prognoses. The theory of the four humours (blood, phlegm, yellow bile and black bile) took shape in this tradition, appearing in On the Nature of Man, and through Galen dominated Western medicine until the 18th century. The Hippocratic Oath, with its pledge to help and do no harm, still echoes in medical ceremonies today.

“Life is short, the art is long.”

Mathematics

Egyptians and Babylonians had sophisticated practical mathematics. The Greek innovation was proof: deriving results by necessary logic from stated assumptions. Thales was credited with the first geometrical theorems. The Pythagoreans discovered, to their dismay according to legend, that the diagonal of a square is incommensurable with its side, an irrational number. Eudoxus of Cnidus (c. 408–355) developed the theory of proportion and the “method of exhaustion,” a precursor of calculus. Euclid’s Elements (c. 300) organized it all into an axiomatic system. Archimedes calculated areas and volumes with near-modern techniques. A lost work of his, The Method, was recovered in 1906 from a palimpsest, a medieval prayer book written over his text. Apollonius of Perga defined the ellipse, parabola and hyperbola, and Diophantus (3rd century CE) pioneered algebraic notation.

Astronomy, geography and biology

By the 5th century BCE Greek thinkers knew the Earth was a sphere. Aristotle gave the evidence: the curved shadow on the Moon during lunar eclipses, and the way stars shift as one travels north or south. Meton described the 19-year lunisolar cycle in 432 BCE. Eudoxus modelled planetary motions with nested spheres, Aristarchus put the Sun at the centre, Hipparchus catalogued the stars, and Claudius Ptolemy (2nd century CE) synthesized geocentric astronomy in the Almagest, the standard text for 1,400 years. In biology, Aristotle’s History of Animals describes hundreds of species from personal observation; his account of the octopus’s reproductive arm was dismissed until the 19th century. His successor Theophrastus did the same for plants and is called the father of botany.

Technology

The Greeks built more than they are often credited with. In the 6th century, Eupalinos dug a 1,036-metre tunnel through a mountain on Samos for an aqueduct, starting from both ends and meeting in the middle. The Diolkos was a paved trackway for dragging ships across the Isthmus of Corinth. Ctesibius of Alexandria built water clocks, a force pump and the water organ. Hero of Alexandria (1st century CE) described automata, a coin-operated holy-water dispenser and the aeolipile, a spinning steam-powered sphere that was treated as a curiosity rather than an engine. And there was the Antikythera mechanism (see The Hellenistic World).

ΙΗ΄

Warfare

Phalanx and Trireme

Two machines made of men: a wall of shields on land, and a bronze-beaked galley rowed by 170 oarsmen at sea.

The hoplite phalanx

The classical hoplite carried a large round shield (aspis, about 90 cm across and 6–8 kg), a thrusting spear (dory, 2–2.5 m) and a short sword. Those who could afford it wore a bronze helmet (the Corinthian type covered the whole face), a bronze or linen-composite cuirass and greaves. His equipment might cost what a labourer earned in months, which is why hoplites came from the propertied classes. In the phalanx, typically eight ranks deep, each man’s shield half-covered the man on his left. Thucydides notes that armies drifted rightward as each man edged toward his neighbour’s shield. Battles were brutal, short and decisive. Scholars still debate how literal the othismos, the mass “shove” of the ranks, was. Sparta’s professional drill gave it an edge for two centuries. Thebes broke that edge with depth and concentration at Leuctra, and the Athenian general Iphicrates showed in 390 BCE how light-armed peltasts could destroy a Spartan regiment that had no missile troops of its own. Philip II’s Macedonian phalanx, with its long sarissa pikes and integrated cavalry, made the old hoplite obsolete.

The trireme

The trireme (trieres) dominated Mediterranean naval warfare from the late 6th to the 4th century BCE. It was a long, light, fast galley, about 37 m long and about 5.5 m wide including the outrigger, rowed by 170 oarsmen in three tiers, one man per oar. Its weapon was a bronze ram (embolon) at the waterline. Tactics were about manoeuvre. In the diekplous ships broke through the enemy line and turned to ram from the side or stern. In the periplous they outflanked it. Rowers were free citizens and hired men, not slaves, contrary to the Hollywood image, and the political clout of Athens’ poorer citizens rested on their oars. In 1987 a full-scale reconstruction, the Greek Navy’s Olympias, was rowed by volunteers and reached about 9 knots in short sprints, confirming much of what ancient texts described.

ΙΘ΄

Legacy

The Long Afterlife of Hellas

“We are all Greeks,” Shelley wrote in 1821. That is an exaggeration, but not a large one.

Democracy. Athenian democracy was direct, not representative. It excluded women, slaves and foreigners, and its critics, Plato foremost, thought it mob rule. Yet the idea that ordinary citizens could govern themselves through open debate, majority vote, equality before the law (isonomia) and equal right to speak (isegoria) passed through the Roman Republic, the Renaissance city-states and Enlightenment thinkers to the founders of modern republics, who argued about Athens and Sparta in their pamphlets.

Western thought. Philosophy, logic, ethics, political theory, history, rhetoric, geometry and scientific inquiry all took their classical forms in Greek. That legacy did not travel in a straight line. It was preserved and extended by Byzantine scholars, translated into Arabic in 8th–10th-century Baghdad (where Aristotle, Euclid, Galen and Ptolemy were studied and commented on by thinkers like al-Kindi, Avicenna and Averroes), and transmitted to Latin Europe in the 12th and 13th centuries and again in the 15th, when Greek scholars fleeing the Ottoman advance helped fuel the Italian Renaissance. Neoclassical architecture, from the British Museum to the U.S. Capitol, and the tragedies still staged worldwide carry it forward.

The Olympics. The ancient Games ran for more than a thousand years, until the late 4th or early 5th century CE. The French educator Pierre de Coubertin revived them, and the first modern Olympics were held in Athens in 1896 in the restored Panathenaic Stadium. The marathon race commemorated the legend of the runner from Marathon, and a Greek water-carrier, Spyridon Louis, won it. The Olympic flame is still lit by the sun’s rays at Olympia.

Κ΄

Epilogue · 330 CE to the present

From Byzantium to the Hellenic Republic

The Greek story did not end with Rome. It continued through a thousand-year Christian empire, four centuries of Ottoman rule, a war of independence and a turbulent modern century.

The eastern Roman Empire, which modern historians call Byzantine but whose people called themselves Romaioi (Romans), was Greek in language and Orthodox Christian in faith. It preserved most of the ancient Greek literature we have today: nearly every classical text survives because Byzantine scribes copied it. When Constantinople fell, the Greek world passed under Ottoman rule, yet the Orthodox Church and the Greek language held Greek identity together until the nationalist revolutions of the 19th century created a modern state that consciously claimed the classical inheritance.

  1. 330 CE

    Constantinople founded

    Constantine dedicates his new capital on the site of Byzantium. Christianity becomes the state religion under Theodosius I (380), who also bans pagan cults. The Olympic Games end around this time.

  2. 529–565

    Justinian

    Justinian I codifies Roman law, builds Hagia Sophia (537) and reconquers Italy and North Africa. In 529 he bars pagans from teaching, traditionally seen as closing the Neoplatonic Academy of Athens. Greek replaces Latin as the language of government in the 7th century.

  3. 7th–11th c.

    Survival and revival

    Arab conquests strip away Egypt and Syria, and Slavic settlement transforms the Balkans. The Macedonian dynasty (867–1056) presides over a cultural renaissance, missionaries Cyril and Methodius carry Orthodoxy and a new alphabet to the Slavs, and the Great Schism with Rome comes in 1054.

  4. 1204

    The Fourth Crusade

    Western crusaders sack Constantinople and carve up the empire into Latin states, including the Duchy of Athens and the Principality of Achaea, while Venice takes Crete and many islands. The Byzantines recover the capital in 1261, but the empire never fully recovers. Late Byzantine Mistras in the Peloponnese becomes a centre of learning: the philosopher Plethon’s Platonism influenced Renaissance Florence.

  5. 1453

    Fall of Constantinople

    On 29 May 1453 the Ottoman sultan Mehmed II takes the city. The last emperor, Constantine XI Palaiologos, dies on the walls. The Morea falls in 1460, Trebizond in 1461, and Venetian Crete only in 1669.

  6. 1453–1821

    The Ottoman centuries

    Greeks live as Orthodox subjects within the Ottoman millet system under the Ecumenical Patriarch. There are heavy taxes and the devshirme levy of boys, but also prosperity for Greek merchants and shipowners and the powerful Phanariot families of Constantinople. In 1687, during a Venetian siege, a Venetian shell ignites Ottoman gunpowder stored in the Parthenon and blows out its centre. Between 1801 and 1812 Lord Elgin removes many of its sculptures to Britain, a dispute that continues today.

  7. 1821–1832

    The War of Independence

    Inspired by the Enlightenment and organized by the secret Filiki Eteria (founded in Odessa in 1814), Greeks rise in revolt. Greeks commemorate 25 March 1821 as the start. Both sides commit massacres. Lord Byron dies at Missolonghi (1824), and the city’s defenders make a desperate breakout in 1826. The destruction of the Ottoman-Egyptian fleet by Britain, France and Russia at Navarino (1827) proves decisive. Ioannis Kapodistrias becomes the first governor (assassinated 1831). Independence is recognized in 1830 and the borders fixed in 1832.

  8. 1833–1913

    Building a kingdom

    Otto of Bavaria becomes king, and the capital moves from Nafplio to Athens (1834). A revolt in 1843 forces a constitution, and Otto is deposed in 1862. Under George I (1863–1913) Britain cedes the Ionian Islands (1864) and Thessaly is added (1881). The “Great Idea” (Megali Idea) of uniting all Greeks drives policy. Athens hosts the first modern Olympics (1896). In the Balkan Wars of 1912–13, led by Prime Minister Eleftherios Venizelos, Greece gains Macedonia with Thessaloniki, Epirus, Crete and the northern Aegean islands.

  9. 1914–1923

    Schism and catastrophe

    The “National Schism” between Venizelos and King Constantine I splits the country during World War I. Greece joins the Allies in 1917. Its army lands at Smyrna in 1919 and pushes deep into Anatolia, but is routed by Mustafa Kemal’s forces in 1922. Smyrna burns, and the Asia Minor Catastrophe ends 3,000 years of Greek life in Anatolia. The Treaty of Lausanne (1923) mandates a population exchange: about 1.2 million Orthodox Christians leave Turkey and roughly 400,000 Muslims leave Greece.

  10. 1924–1940

    Republic and dictatorship

    A republic (1924–35) is followed by the restoration of the monarchy and, from 4 August 1936, the dictatorship of Ioannis Metaxas.

  11. 1940–1944

    War and occupation

    On 28 October 1940 Greece rejects Mussolini’s ultimatum. The day is still celebrated as Ochi (“No”) Day. The Greek army drives the Italians back into Albania, but Germany invades in April 1941 and takes Crete in May. A triple German, Italian and Bulgarian occupation follows. The famine of 1941–42 kills tens of thousands, and reprisal massacres devastate villages. About 85–90 percent of Greek Jews are murdered in the Holocaust, including almost all of Thessaloniki’s historic Sephardic community. A powerful resistance grows, dominated by the communist-led EAM/ELAS. Liberation comes in October 1944.

  12. 1946–1949

    Civil war

    Fighting in Athens in December 1944 escalates into full civil war between the government, backed by Britain and then by the U.S. under the Truman Doctrine (1947), and the communist Democratic Army. It ends in government victory in 1949, leaving deep scars, mass emigration and decades of political polarization. Greece joins NATO in 1952, and the Dodecanese pass from Italy to Greece in 1947.

  13. 1967–1974

    The Colonels’ junta

    On 21 April 1967 a group of army colonels seizes power. Students rise at the Athens Polytechnic on 17 November 1973. The junta collapses in July 1974 after sponsoring a coup in Cyprus that triggers a Turkish invasion and the continuing division of the island.

  14. 1974–2008

    Metapolitefsi and Europe

    Konstantinos Karamanlis restores democracy, and a referendum in December 1974 abolishes the monarchy. The 1975 constitution founds the Third Hellenic Republic. Greece joins the European Community on 1 January 1981, the year Andreas Papandreou’s PASOK comes to power. It adopts the euro in 2001 and hosts the 2004 Athens Olympics.

  15. 2009–2018

    The debt crisis

    Revelations of a far larger deficit than reported trigger a sovereign debt crisis. Three international bailouts (2010, 2012 and 2015) come with harsh austerity. The economy shrinks by roughly a quarter and unemployment peaks above 27 percent. In 2015 the left-wing Syriza government of Alexis Tsipras calls a referendum, and voters reject the creditors’ terms, yet a third bailout follows within weeks. Greece exits its last programme in August 2018. The Prespa Agreement (2018) resolves the long dispute with North Macedonia over that country’s name.

  16. 2019–2025

    Greece today

    Under Prime Minister Kyriakos Mitsotakis (New Democracy, elected 2019, re-elected 2023) the economy recovers. Greece regains an investment-grade credit rating in 2023–24, repays bailout loans early and enjoys record tourism. It also faces deadly wildfires, migration pressures, the 2023 Tempi rail disaster and the Pylos migrant shipwreck. In 2024 it became the first Orthodox-majority country to legalize same-sex civil marriage. The population was about 10.4 million at the 2021 census and is ageing. (This summary reflects events through roughly 2025, and very recent developments may not be captured.)

Modern Greeks live among the ruins that the world treats as its common heritage, and they still speak a language that a reader of Plato can partly recognize. The road from Knossos to modern Athens is long and broken, and its continuities have sometimes been exaggerated for political ends. They are still real. The language, the landscape, the Orthodox Church’s Byzantine inheritance and the habit of arguing about the polis have all lasted for some four thousand years.
