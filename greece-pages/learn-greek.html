Μάθε ΕλληνικάLearn Greek, letter by letter.

01 · History Ιστορία

3,400 years of writing Greek

Greek has the longest documented history of any living Indo-European language. Its alphabet — the first to write vowels as full letters — became the ancestor of the Latin letters you're reading now and of the Cyrillic used from Sofia to Vladivostok.

c. 1450–1200 BCE

Linear B — before the alphabet

Mycenaean palace scribes wrote an early form of Greek in a syllabary of roughly 87 signs (plus picture-like ideograms) on clay tablets at Knossos, Pylos and Mycenae. Michael Ventris deciphered it in 1952, showing it was Greek.

c. 1200–800 BCE

Writing goes quiet

When the palace civilisation collapsed, Linear B vanished from the Greek mainland. (On Cyprus a related syllabary lived on for centuries.)

8th century BCE

Borrowing from the Phoenicians 𐤀𐤁𐤂

Greeks adapted the Phoenician script, which wrote only consonants. Their stroke of genius: reusing letters for sounds Greek lacked as vowels — ʾālep → Α, hē → Ε, ḥēt → Η, yōd → Ι, ʿayin → Ο, wāw → Υ. Early examples include Nestor's Cup from Pithekoussai.

8th–5th c. BCE

Local alphabets, East and West

Each city had its own variant, adding Φ, Χ, Ψ and (in Ionia) Ω. A western variant travelled via Cumae in Italy to the Etruscans and then the Romans: the Latin alphabet.

403/402 BCE

Athens standardises

Under the archon Eucleides, Athens officially adopted the 24-letter Ionic alphabet — the same 24 letters used today.

c. 200 BCE

Accent marks invented

Scholars in Alexandria, traditionally Aristophanes of Byzantium, introduced accents and breathings to help readers pronounce Homer.

9th–10th c. CE

Lowercase & Cyrillic

Byzantine scribes developed the fast minuscule hand that became our lowercase letters. In the First Bulgarian Empire, followers of Saints Cyril and Methodius built the Cyrillic script largely on Greek uncial letters.

1976 / 1982

Modern reforms

Demotic (everyday) Greek became the official language in 1976, and in 1982 the monotonic system replaced the old accents with a single stress mark, the τόνος.

Why learn the alphabet first?

  • It's small and it's phonetic. 24 letters plus about a dozen letter pairs. Once you know them, you can read almost any word aloud correctly.
  • Stress is written for you. Every word of more than one syllable carries an accent mark showing which syllable to stress.
  • You already know a third of it. Α, Β, Ε, Ζ, Ι, Κ, Μ, Ν, Ο, Τ, Χ look familiar, and π, Σ, Δ, Ω, μ appear in maths and science.
  • Transliteration is a trap. Greek has no single standard way to write it in Latin letters. Reading the real script from day one saves you from unlearning later.
  • Instant rewards. Shop signs, street names, menus and thousands of English cognates (τηλέφωνο, μουσική, κέντρο) open up straight away.
Modern or Ancient? This page teaches Modern Greek first — the living language — and switches to Ancient & Koine pronunciation whenever you flip the toggle. A dedicated Ancient & Koine section covers polytonic accents, Homer and the New Testament.

    05 · Sounds Οι ήχοι

    Letter pairs, stress & pronunciation

    Modern Greek spelling preserves ancient spelling, so several letter pairs (digraphs) stand for a single sound. Learn these and you can read nearly anything aloud.

    Vowel pairs

    The αυ / ευ rule

    In αυ and ευ, the υ turns into a consonant:

    • av / ev before a vowel or a voiced consonant (β γ δ ζ λ μ ν ρ): αυγό avghó “egg”, Ευρώπη Evrópi “Europe”.
    • af / ef before a voiceless consonant (θ κ ξ π σ τ φ χ ψ) or at the end of a word: αυτό aftó “this”, ευχαριστώ efharistó “thank you”.
    • With a diaeresis or an accent on the α/ε, the letters are read separately: άυλος áilos “immaterial”.

    Consonant pairs

    Six ways to write “ee”

    Ancient Greek had six different sounds here. Over the centuries they all merged into one [i] — but the spellings stayed. (Modern Greek also has two ways to write “o” (ο, ω) and two ways to write “e” (ε, αι).)

    [i] — “ee” as in seeone sound, six spellings: ι · η · υ · ει · οι · υι

    Stress: the τόνος and the diaeresis

    Every Modern Greek word of two or more syllables has exactly one stressed syllable, marked with the τόνος (´) on its vowel: καλημέρα kaliméra. One-syllable words usually go without it, except a few that would otherwise be confused: ή “or” vs η “the”, and πού; “where?” vs που “that/which”. On capitals the mark sits to the upper left (Άννα), and ALL-CAPS text usually drops it.

    Stress changes meaning. Listen:

    The diaeresis (¨, διαλυτικά) tells you to read two vowels separately rather than as a pair:

    καιke — “and” (αι = e)
    παϊδάκιαpa-i-dhákia — lamb chops (α + ϊ)
    κοροϊδεύωkoro-i-dhévo — I tease
    πρωτεΐνηprote-íni — protein (ΐ = stress + diaeresis)
    τσάιtsá-i — tea (the accent on α already splits the pair, so no ¨ is needed)

    Pronunciation guide

    Five pure vowels

    Greek has just five vowel sounds: a (father), e (met), i (see), o (or, short), u (moon). They never glide like English “oh-oo” and never weaken to “uh”, even unstressed. Αθήνα is a-THÍ-na, never “uh-THEE-nuh”.

    Throaty consonants

    γ is a voiced gargle “gh” (like a soft French r) before a, o, u and consonants, and “y” before e/i sounds: γάλα ghála, γεια ya. χ is the “kh” of loch, softening to the h of huge before e/i: χέρι héri.

    Two different “th”s

    θ = think (voiceless) and δ = this (voiced). Contrast θέμα théma “topic” with δέμα dhéma “parcel”.

    Tapped r, crisp stops

    ρ is a quick tongue tap like Spanish pero (rolled when doubled or for emphasis). π, τ, κ are unaspirated, with no puff of air, as in English spin, stop, skin.

    Double letters are single

    θάλασσα is thálasa and Άννα is Ána. (Cypriot Greek does lengthen doubled consonants.)

    Little assimilations

    σ becomes [z] before voiced consonants: κόσμος kózmos “world”. A final ν blends with a following π/τ/κ: τον πατέρα → tom batéra. κ, γ, χ, λ, ν soften (palatalise) before i/e sounds: ήλιος ≈ ílyos.

    Transliteration on this page: dh = δ (this), th = θ (think), gh = γ (gargled), h = χ (throaty), y = γ before e/i, á é í ó ú = stressed vowel.

    07 · Grammar primer Γραμματική

    The essentials, gently

    Greek grammar has a reputation, but the core is very regular. Nouns have a gender and change their endings by case, and verbs change their endings by person. That's why Greek can drop subject pronouns and shuffle word order freely.

    Three genders, three articles

    ο

    Masculine

    Often ends in -ος, -ας, -ης:
    ο δρόμος the road · ο πατέρας the father · ο μαθητής the pupil

    η

    Feminine

    Often ends in -α, -η:
    η θάλασσα the sea · η πόλη the city · η μητέρα the mother

    το

    Neuter

    Often ends in -ο, -ι, -μα:
    το βουνό the mountain · το παιδί the child · το γράμμα the letter

    Gender is grammatical, not logical (το κορίτσι “the girl” is neuter), and there are exceptions such as feminine η οδός “the street”. Always learn a noun together with its article. Indefinite article: ένας / μία (μια) / ένα “a/one”.

    The four cases

    Nominative

    The subject. Ο φίλος είναι εδώ. “The friend is here.”

    Genitive

    Possession (“of”), and unstressed pronouns for “to him/her”. το σπίτι του φίλου “the friend's house”.

    Accusative

    The direct object, and after most prepositions. Βλέπω τον φίλο. “I see the friend.”

    Vocative

    Calling or addressing someone. Φίλε, έλα! “Friend, come!” · Νίκο!

    Word order: why Greek can shuffle and English can't

    English
    Greek
    In English, position shows who does what. In Greek, the nominative (ο σκύλος) and accusative (τον άνθρωπο) endings carry that information, so the meaning survives any order. Subject–verb–object is still the neutral default.
    Questions use ; — the Greek question mark looks like an English semicolon. Μιλάς ελληνικά; Milás eliniká? “Do you speak Greek?” Same word order as the statement; only the rising intonation changes. The Greek semicolon is a raised dot: ·
    Subjects are optional. The verb ending already says who: Μιλάω ελληνικά. “I speak Greek.” Negate with δεν: Δεν μιλάω ελληνικά. “I don't speak Greek.”

    Counting in Greek

    1, 3 and 4 change with gender (ένας/μία/ένα, τρεις/τρία, τέσσερις/τέσσερα). The neuter form, used for counting, is shown here. Where two forms exist (επτά/εφτά), both are correct.

    Tens and beyond

    Bonus: the Greek alphabetic numerals

    Before Arabic numerals, Greeks wrote numbers with letters followed by a keraia (ʹ). You still see them for book chapters, school grades, kings and popes: Αʹ Λυκείου (first year of upper secondary school), Κωνσταντίνος Βʹ (Constantine II). Thousands take a low mark in front: ͵α = 1000.

    11 · Ancient & Koine Αρχαία & Κοινή

    Reading Homer and the Gospels

    Classical (Attic) Greek is the language of 5th-century BCE Athens. Homer's epic dialect is older, and Koine (“common”) Greek is the international language of the Hellenistic and Roman worlds, used in the New Testament. Same alphabet, but the spelling uses the polytonic system and the sound was quite different.

    Polytonic marks: accents & breathings

    ἀ
    Smooth breathing
    ψιλή — no h-sound. Written on every word beginning with a vowel that lacks an h.
    ἁ
    Rough breathing
    δασεῖα — adds an “h”: ἁμαρτία hamartía, ὁ ho. Initial ρ and υ always take it: ῥ, ὑ.
    ά
    Acute
    ὀξεῖα — originally a rising pitch.
    ὰ
    Grave
    βαρεῖα — replaces a final acute when another word follows: θεὰ, καὶ.
    ᾶ
    Circumflex
    περισπωμένη — rise-and-fall pitch, on long vowels and diphthongs only: ῆ, ῶ.
    ᾳ
    Iota subscript
    ὑπογεγραμμένη — a silent “little iota” under ᾳ ῃ ῳ: ἀρχῇ.
    ἄ ἅ ῆ
    Combined
    A breathing goes before or under the accent: ἄ ἅ ἂ ἃ ἆ ἇ ᾄ.
    ϊ ΐ
    Diaeresis
    διαλυτικά — read the vowels separately: Πηληϊάδεω.

    Homer, Iliad 1.1–2

    “Sing, goddess, the wrath of Achilles, son of Peleus — the accursed wrath that laid countless woes upon the Achaeans…”

    Epic dialect, about the 8th century BCE. The very first word is μῆνιν, “wrath”: the theme of the whole poem. The elision mark (’) shows a dropped final vowel, as in μυρί’ for μυρία.

    Gospel of John 1:1 (Koine)

    “In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.”

    Notice the grave accents (καὶ, πρὸς, τὸν, θεὸς) where an acute stands before another word, the iota subscript in ἀρχῇ (dative case), and the rough breathing on ὁ “the”. Speak buttons use a Modern Greek voice, which is close to how Greek Orthodox churches read these texts today.

    Ancient vs Modern at a glance

    Cases

    Ancient has five cases including the dative (“to/for/with”). Modern has four. The dative survives only in fixed expressions: εντάξει “OK” (from ἐν τάξει, “in order”).

    Verbs

    Ancient had infinitives, the optative mood and a dual number. Modern Greek uses να + verb instead of the infinitive (θέλω να γράψω “I want to write”) and builds the future with θα.

    Vocabulary

    Many everyday words were replaced: water ὕδωρ → νερό, bread ἄρτος → ψωμί, wine οἶνος → κρασί, horse ἵππος → άλογο, fish ἰχθύς → ψάρι. The old words live on in compounds (υδραυλικός “plumber”, ιππόδρομος “racecourse”).

    Spelling

    Polytonic (three accents, two breathings, iota subscript) vs monotonic (one τόνος, since 1982). Spelling is otherwise largely unchanged, so a Modern reader can sound out Plato.

    Sound

    Pitch accent became stress, vowel length disappeared, the h-sound vanished, and six vowel spellings merged into [i] (see above).

    Mutual intelligibility

    A Modern speaker recognises much of the New Testament, a fair amount of Plato with effort, and Homer only with study, roughly like an English speaker reading the King James Bible, Chaucer and Beowulf.

    12 · Where it's spoken Πού μιλιέται

    Greece, Cyprus & a global diaspora

    Greek is the official language of Greece (about 10 million people) and, with Turkish, of the Republic of Cyprus. It's one of the EU's 24 official languages, and a recognised minority language in Albania, Italy, Armenia, Hungary, Romania and Ukraine. Large diaspora communities live in Australia, the USA, Canada, Germany and the UK; Melbourne is often said to have one of the largest Greek-speaking populations outside Greece and Cyprus.
