01 — The substrate

Four letters, twenty amino acids

Every model on this page is ultimately a model of the same thing: a one-dimensional string that a cell turns into a three-dimensional machine. DNA is transcribed into mRNA; ribosomes read that mRNA three letters at a time and chain together amino acids. That mapping — the genetic code — is nearly universal across life, which is exactly why a model trained on bacteria can say something useful about you. Type a sequence and watch it run.

Why this matters for AI

Because biology is written in discrete symbols, nearly every trick from language modelling transfers. A protein is a sentence in a 20-letter alphabet. A genome is a document in a 4-letter alphabet. Models like ESM and Evo are literally next-token or masked-token predictors — the novelty is the corpus, not the architecture.

The catch: the central dogma is a lossy summary. Splicing, regulation, modification and folding all happen between the letters and the function, and most of the hard remaining problems in AI-for-biology live in those gaps.

That last number is the whole game. Evolution has sampled a vanishing fraction of protein space; generative models are an attempt to search the rest of it on purpose.
02 — Structure prediction

From a string to a shape

A protein's function follows from its 3D fold, and for fifty years predicting that fold from sequence was a grand challenge. At CASP14 in 2020, AlphaFold2 reached accuracy competitive with experiment for most targets, a result recognised by the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry (Hassabis and Jumper, shared with David Baker for protein design). Reading the colours

pLDDT is AlphaFold's per-residue guess at its own accuracy. Helix cores score high (dark blue); loops less; the floppy termini score low. A low score is often a true statement about biology: about a third of the human proteome is predicted to contain intrinsically disordered regions that have no single fold.

The toy here interpolates a chain into a hand-built bundle, so it shows what a prediction looks like, not how physics folds a chain. Real folding is a search over an astronomically large landscape; AlphaFold sidesteps the search by learning the answer from ~200k solved structures.

03 — Protein language models

Learning biology by filling in blanks

Hide 15% of the amino acids in millions of natural proteins, train a transformer to guess them back, and something remarkable happens: the model learns which residues touch in 3D, which mutations are tolerated, and how families are related — without ever seeing a structure. That is the recipe behind ESM-2, ESM C, ESM3 and ProGen3.

Why masking works

To predict a hidden residue well, the model must know what the protein needs there: a buried hydrophobic, a glycine in a tight turn, a charged residue that pairs with a partner 40 positions away. Over billions of examples those constraints become the model's internal representation.

Scale helps. Bigger models put sharper probability on the right residue, and their embeddings predict structure better. Profluent reported wet-lab evidence of scaling benefits with ProGen3 (up to 46B parameters, trained on 3.4B proteins). The toggle above mimics that sharpening.

The demo is a hand-tuned stand-in for a real model (it knows residue classes and neighbours, not evolution), but the training objective it illustrates is exact.

04 — Generative protein design

Sculpting proteins out of noise

Structure prediction maps sequence → shape. Design runs it backwards. RFdiffusion (Baker lab) borrows the trick behind image generators: corrupt real protein backbones with noise, train a network to undo it, then start from pure noise and denoise into a brand-new backbone — optionally constrained to grab a target or hold an active site. ProteinMPNN then writes a sequence that folds into it.

Reverse diffusion

t = 1000 · pure noise

The design loop, 2026 edition

1 · Backbone. A diffusion model generates a backbone, conditioned on a target surface (binders), a fixed motif (scaffolding) or nothing at all.

2 · Sequence. An inverse-folding model such as ProteinMPNN proposes sequences for that backbone.

3 · Filter. A structure predictor refolds each sequence. Designs that come back in the same shape with high confidence are kept — the "self-consistency" check.

4 · Lab. Dozens, not millions, get synthesised. Recent tools report double-digit experimental hit rates: BindCraft (10–100% across targets, Nature 2025), Chai-2 for zero-shot antibodies, and Latent-X testing just 30–100 designs per target.

The all-atom turn

The field has moved from backbones to every atom. RFdiffusion2 (Nature Methods, Dec 2025) scaffolds enzyme active sites straight from catalytic geometry and solved all 41 benchmark sites versus 16 previously. RFdiffusion3 (Science, 2026) diffuses free atoms around ligands, DNA and RNA and is reported as ~10× faster than RFD2. BoltzGen (MIT, Oct 2025) unified design and folding in one open model.
05 — DNA foundation models

Reading the 98% that isn't genes

Only about 1–2% of the human genome codes for protein. The rest holds switches — promoters, enhancers, splice signals — whose grammar we barely understand, and most disease-associated variants land there. Two model families attack it: sequence-to-function predictors such as AlphaGenome, and genomic language models such as Evo 2. Introduce a variant and watch the predicted readouts move.

AlphaGenome Google DeepMind · Nature, Jan 2026

Takes 1 Mb of DNA and predicts thousands of genomic tracks at up to single-base resolution: expression, chromatin accessibility, histone marks, TF binding, 3D contacts and splice junctions. It matched or beat the strongest external models on 25 of 26 variant-effect evaluations. In September 2026 DeepMind released AlphaGenome Atlas: predictions for all ~9 billion possible single-letter human variants, a ~1-petabyte resource free for academic research.

Evo 2 Arc Institute + NVIDIA · Nature, Mar 2026

A 40-billion-parameter genomic language model with a 1-million-token context, trained on roughly 9 trillion nucleotides spanning bacteria, archaea, phages and eukaryotes. With no task-specific training it scores variant pathogenicity, including clinically important BRCA1 variants, simply by asking how surprised it is by the mutated sequence. It can also generate genome-scale sequences. Weights, code and data are fully open.

06 — Single-cell & the virtual cell

Simulating a cell before touching a pipette

Single-cell sequencing measures every gene in every individual cell, so tissues become clouds of points in expression space. The dream is a virtual cell: a model that predicts how that cloud shifts when you knock out a gene or add a drug. Pick a gene below and run a perturbation on a simulated bone-marrow atlas.

Where the field really is

Foundation models such as scGPT and Geneformer were pretrained on tens of millions of cells. Yet a 2025 Nature Methods benchmark found that none of five foundation models beat deliberately simple baselines — predicting the training mean, or a linear model — at predicting unseen perturbations.

The response has been better data and harder tests. Arc Institute's State (Cell, Aug 2026) was trained on 167M observational and 100M+ perturbed cells and reports clear gains over baselines on Tahoe-100M. The 2026 Virtual Cell Challenge now demands zero-shot prediction in six never-perturbed cell lines.

07 — Pathology & radiology

Where AI already ships

Imaging is the most mature corner of medical AI. Of the 1,614 authorisation records on the FDA's AI-enabled device list (decisions through June 2026), about 76% went through the radiology panel. Pathology is catching up as glass slides are digitised into gigapixel images. Toggle the model's attention to see where it looked.

Procedurally generated images, not real patients. Hover a patch for its attention weight.

Top-attended patches

How slide-level models see

A whole-slide image can exceed 100,000 × 100,000 pixels, far too big for one forward pass. So the slide is tiled into patches, each one embedded by a ViT foundation model pretrained with self-supervision on millions of slides. Paige's dataset, acquired by Tempus in August 2025, spans almost 7 million slides.

An attention-based MIL head then weighs every patch and pools them into one diagnosis. Those weights are the heatmap. They show where the model looked, which is not the same as why it decided.

In radiology the frontier is one model covering many findings. Aidoc's CARE foundation model was cleared in 2026 for a CT triage workflow with 11 new indications, and has Breakthrough designations for chest X-ray triage and draft reports.

08 — Clinical language models

The scribe, the second opinion, and the patient's phone

The fastest-adopted medical AI of 2024–2026 doesn't diagnose anything. Ambient scribes listen to the visit and draft the note, attacking the documentation burden behind clinician burnout. Diagnosis-support systems are close behind, and in January 2026 both OpenAI (ChatGPT Health, ChatGPT for Healthcare) and Anthropic (Claude for Healthcare) launched dedicated health products.

Evidence check

Microsoft MAI-DxO (Science, Oct 2026) runs a panel of virtual physicians that ask questions, order tests and debate. On 304 NEJM case challenges it reached 83% accuracy versus 22–36% for physicians, at lower test cost. Physicians were restricted in ways real practice is not, and these are curated puzzles, not routine care.

Google's AMIE now runs live video consultations and matched or beat primary-care physicians in a randomised study with patient actors. Google is explicit that real-patient studies must come first. Open models such as MedGemma 1.5 (Jan 2026) let hospitals build and validate their own tools.

09 — The pipeline

Ten years, a billion dollars, nine failures in ten

Bringing a new drug to approval is commonly cited as taking 10–15 years and $1–2.6B per approved drug once failures are counted, and roughly 90% of candidates entering human trials fail. AI's strongest evidence so far is in the early, preclinical stages. Clinical trials still move at the speed of biology and patients. Click a stage to see where AI plugs in.

What the clinical evidence says

A 2024 analysis of AI-native biotechs (Jayatunga et al., Drug Discovery Today) found 21 of 24 AI-discovered molecules passed Phase I, an 80–90% success rate against historical ~40–65%. Phase II was ~40% (4 of 10), in line with industry. A broader 2026 census counted 129 AI-enabled assets that had entered trials by March 2026, with observed success of 77.8% in Phase I and 19.5% in Phase II. Only five had reached Phase 3.

The reading: AI is good at making molecules that behave like drugs. It hasn't yet shown it can pick biology that works in patients.

The wet-lab bottleneck

Internet text is effectively free; biological data is not. A protein measurement can cost dollars to thousands of dollars and take days. So the frontier is closing the loop: models choose the most informative experiments, robots run them, and results flow back into training.

Examples: Lila Sciences builds "AI Science Factories"; Periodic Labs treats nature as the reinforcement-learning environment; Lilly's TuneLab offers models trained on proprietary data it says cost over $1B to generate, through federated learning; and OpenFold3 was co-trained by pharma consortium members without pooling raw data.

Proprietary data deals now set the pace: Genesis–Incyte, Inceptive–Alnylam, Chai–Lilly and Boltz–Pfizer all include model training on the partner's private data.

14 — Reality check

What the models can't do (yet)

Every section above shows real progress, and every one of them has a catch. The honest summary for October 2026: AI is very good at proposing molecules, structures and hypotheses, and still unproven at the part that matters most — showing that those proposals help patients. Each card pairs a common claim with the reality. Click to expand.

Hype vs. reality pipeline

"AI has solved drug discovery"
AI-enabled molecules pass Phase I more often than average, but Phase II success looks like the industry's. The most advanced AI-designed drugs entered Phase 3 only in 2026.

Faster and cheaper preclinical work is real. But most of the time and money in drug development goes to clinical trials, and most failures happen there because the biological hypothesis (the target) was wrong, not because the molecule was badly made. Until AI-picked targets succeed in late-stage trials, claims of a revolution are premature.

Failed trials happen evidence

"AI-designed means de-risked"
Several early AI-derived drugs have been discontinued or missed their endpoints, which is normal for drug development and a useful corrective.

Examples include DSP-1181, among the first AI-designed molecules to enter human trials (2020), later discontinued; a BenevolentAI eczema candidate that missed efficacy goals in Phase 2a (2023); and several Recursion programs cut in 2025 after disappointing data. None of this means AI doesn't work. It means AI hasn't repealed biology.

Benchmark leakage evaluation

"State of the art on the benchmark"
When test examples resemble training examples, scores overstate how well a model will do on genuinely new problems.

Leakage is common in biology because the same public databases feed both training and testing. Look for time-split or family-split evaluations, blind challenges such as CASP and the Virtual Cell Challenge, and prospective tests. Simple baselines matter too: the 2025 perturbation benchmark showed elaborate models losing to a linear one.

Data bias & representativeness fairness

"Trained on millions of patients"
Millions of records from a narrow slice of humanity can still fail everyone else. Genomic datasets are dominated by people of European ancestry.

Variant-effect scores, polygenic risk scores, dermatology classifiers and imaging models have all shown weaker performance in under-represented groups. Scanner vendors, hospital workflows and labelling habits also differ between sites, so a model validated at one hospital can drift at the next. The fix is unglamorous: diverse data collection, subgroup reporting and local validation.

Privacy trust

"The data is de-identified"
Genomes are inherently identifying, and clinical notes are full of personal detail. Removing names doesn't make records anonymous.

Federated learning (training where the data lives), secure enclaves, consent-aware data use and strong access controls are becoming standard for health AI. Ambient scribes raise their own questions about recording consent and data retention, and health chatbots for consumers sit partly outside the rules that govern hospitals.

Regulation & validation clinic

"FDA-cleared, so proven to help"
Clearance shows a device is reasonably safe and performs as claimed. It usually doesn't show that patients end up better off.

Most AI devices on the FDA list were cleared by showing they are substantially equivalent to an existing device, and relatively few have been tested in prospective randomised trials. Regulators are adapting: the FDA now accepts predetermined change-control plans for models that are updated, and the EU AI Act treats most medical AI as high-risk, with obligations phasing in over the next few years. General-purpose chatbots used for health questions remain a grey zone.

Structure ≠ function biology

"We know the shape, so we know what it does"
A predicted structure is a single static snapshot. Proteins move, switch shapes, bind partners and get modified inside living cells.

Structure predictors can confidently predict the same fold for a mutant that has lost all activity. They also struggle with binding strength, conformational change and disordered regions. A designed binder that looks perfect on screen still has to be made, tested for activity, specificity and stability, and shown to work in cells and animals. Most designs don't.

Dual use & governance responsibility

"Open models are always good"
Powerful biological design tools could in principle be misused. The field is building safeguards while trying to keep the benefits of open science.

Responses include community commitments to responsible AI in protein design, screening of DNA synthesis orders, filtering of sensitive training data, staged or access-controlled model releases, and government guidance and evaluations. Reasonable people disagree about where to draw lines between openness and caution, and that debate is part of doing this work responsibly.

How to read an AI-in-biology headline. Ask four questions: Was it tested on data truly unlike the training set? Was it confirmed in a wet lab or a clinic, not just in silico? Who was in the dataset? And did anything improve for patients, or only on a benchmark?
What would change the picture. A run of Phase 3 successes for AI-chosen targets, prospective trials showing that clinical AI improves outcomes rather than just accuracy, and virtual-cell models that beat simple baselines on never-seen perturbations. Several of those readouts are due in 2027–2028.
